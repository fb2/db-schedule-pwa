# 4. Mamak, Chulia, Chettiar, Hadhrami

Two things are usually said about Indian food in Penang. The first is that mamak stalls sell
adapted Chinese noodles. The second is that "mamak" means Indian. The first is true and more
interesting than it sounds; the second is wrong in a way that matters, because the crucial
distinction in Penang is not Chinese-versus-Indian, it is **Indian Muslim versus Indian**.

## The word

**Mamak** is from Tamil **மாமா** *māmā*, "maternal uncle" — a respectful address for an older
man, extended in Malaysian and Singaporean usage to shopkeepers and stallholders generally,
and thence to a Tamil Muslim person and to the stall type itself. The OED records all three
senses. In Melaka court usage the Tamil *mama* had already been adopted as an honorific for
Indian Muslims who married into the royal family. And Malaysian children of every ethnicity
are taught to call adult strangers uncle and auntie, which is the pragmatic bridge that made
the extension natural.

Note that Wiktionary records a competing derivation from Proto-Malayic for the *Malay*
"uncle," so the deeper kinship term may be inherited while the Tamil-Muslim and stall senses
are a contact development. The term is also contested in contemporary Malaysian discourse as
potentially derogatory — a Cambridge University Press chapter is titled, pointedly, "'Mamak'
and Malaysian: The Indian Muslim Quest for Identity." Worth knowing before using it casually
in print.

## Who: the Chulias and their sub-communities

The East India Company term **Chulia** referred to Indians from the Coromandel coast of Tamil
Nadu, conventionally connected to the Chola country. Tamil Muslim traders had been operating
along the Kedah coast for centuries before 1786 and arrived in Penang within days of Light's
landing. **Lebuh Chulia was originally Malabar Street**, after the Kerala Muslims who settled
there, and was renamed Chulia Street in 1798 as Tamil settlement grew. The 1833 census
recorded 7,886 Chulias in Penang and 510 in Province Wellesley.

The sub-communities matter because they carry different foodways:

- **Marakkayar** — a maritime trading community of the Coromandel ports (Nagapattinam, Nagore,
  Kilakarai, Kayalpatnam) tracing descent from Arab seafarers who married local South Indian
  women. Arabic-Persian-inflected, mercantile, and dominant in Penang's early Indian Muslim
  leadership. **Cauder Mohideen Merican**, a Marakkayar, was appointed the first *Kapitan
  Keling* in 1801 on land granted by Leith that became the Kapitan Keling Mosque. The
  "Merican" surname in Malaysia is a Marakkayar marker. Note the recursion: this community was
  *already* an Arab-Indian creole one step upstream of Penang's own creolisation.
- **Rawther** and **Labbai** — other Tamil Muslim endogamous groups, more associated with
  inland trade and religious learning respectively.
- **Mappila / Malabar Muslims** from Kerala — the Malabar Street layer, and the likely conduit
  for the *Malabar parotta* lineage that becomes roti canai.

The **Nagore Durgha Sheriff** on Chulia Street, built around 1801–1803, is the oldest Indian
Muslim shrine on the island, dedicated to the Sufi saint Syed Shahul Hamid of Nagore and built
by Tamil Muslim traders from Thanjavur who carried his cult with them. It remains one of the
few surviving specimens of early nineteenth-century South Indian Muslim architecture in George
Town.

## Nasi kandar, and the shoulder pole that shaped a serving style

**Nasi kandar** is genuinely Penang's, not generic Malaysia's, and it is the best single
illustration of how a delivery mechanism becomes a cuisine.

The name comes from the ***kandar***, the shoulder pole from which itinerant Indian Muslim
vendors slung two containers — one of rice, one of curries — hawking to the dock labour of
**Weld Quay**. The etymology given is Hindi/Urdu *kandha*, shoulder. And the dish form — a
plate of rice flooded with *multiple* curry gravies deliberately allowed to run together,
*kuah campur*, "banjir," flooded — is a mobile-vendor economy fossilised into a service style.
One man, two pots, no plating station: of course the gravies mix. Vendors progressed from
poles to bicycles to trishaws to municipal shoplots, and the flood stayed.

The same apparatus is why **mee rebus** matters to this chapter. Singapore's National Library
opens its account with a flat commitment: mee rebus "was originally peddled by Indian Muslim
immigrants," cooking to order from a mobile stove and containers slung on a shoulder pole,
carried south from the northern states. That is the strongest single attribution the dish has —
stronger than the Javanese derivation it is more usually given.

And the *kandar* pole shows up a third time, seven hundred kilometres down the Straits, in the
founding story of bak chor mee: Chen Lianfu hawked from a two-basket shoulder-pole stall in Chai Chee
following street-opera crowds, and the load bent his back until his stall was known as
驼背面, "Hunchback Noodle." Different community, different dish, same technology, same
consequence: the equipment determines the format.

## How mamak cooks rebuilt Chinese noodles

Here is the mechanism, and it is systematic rather than accidental. Tamil Muslim cooks took
**Chinese noodle formats** — yellow alkaline wheat *mee*, bean sprouts, tofu, soy sauce, wok
stir-frying — and rebuilt them **halal** and **South Indian-spiced**, then added Malay and
Western industrial elements.

Keep the noodle, the taugeh, the tofu, the egg and the wok. Remove the pork and the lard. Add
sambal or chilli paste, curry leaf, garam masala, potato, tamarind — and, from the mid
twentieth century, bottled tomato and chilli sauce, which is what gives mee goreng mamak its
distinctive red-orange.

**Mee goreng mamak** is the flagship, and its name is the most efficient artefact in this
entire project: *mee* from Hokkien 麵, *goreng* Malay for fried, *mamak* from Tamil for
maternal uncle. **Chinese noun, Malay verb, Tamil honorific — the whole fusion encoded in four
syllables.** Penang is credited as the origin, with Bangkok Lane Mee Goreng in Pulau Tikus
cited among the oldest, from a pushcart, now over ninety years running. The Penang style is
notably *wet* — gravy is added, and at Bangkok Lane it is borrowed from the pasembur stall
next door, which is hybridisation you can stand and watch.

The dish cannot be found in India, and the community distinction is doing real work. Tamil
**Hindu** migration went overwhelmingly into estate labour under indenture and the *kangani*
system, with a food repertoire shaped by widespread vegetarianism and beef avoidance. Tamil
**Muslim** migration was mercantile and urban, in George Town from the earliest years — and
being Muslim, they could **sell to Malays** and **buy Chinese noodles**, wheat and alkali and
no pork. Halal is not an incidental attribute of this cuisine. It is the *enabling constraint*
that created the mamak stall as an inter-communal institution, occupying the one commercial
position neither Chinese nor Malay hawkers could hold.

The rest of the mamak noodle repertoire follows from the same logic. **Maggi goreng** is mee
goreng mamak with the fresh noodle swapped for a rehydrated instant block — and it is the only
dish in this graph with a supply-chain birthday, since Maggi reached Malaysia in 1969 with
tomato ketchup and chilli sauce and only in 1971 with two-minute noodles. Pause on that
sequence: the bottled sauce that defines the dish's colour arrived two years before the noodle
that defines its name. **Mee sotong** is a Penang-only mamak invention, squid sliced thin and
cooked *into* a dark sweet-spicy sauce rather than laid on top; the reference stall is Hameed
"Pata" at Padang Kota Lama, where the family's account has the father selling mee rebus and
mee goreng from 1942 and the mee sotong specialisation dating to 1978. **Bihun goreng mamak**
is the rice-vermicelli sibling. **Mee soto** and **mee rebus** are Javanese and Malay in origin
but core mamak items, generally more chilli-forward and less coconut-sweet in mamak hands.

And **pasembur** — Indian rojak — sits beside all of it: shredded cucumber and *sengkuang*,
bean sprouts, potato, boiled egg, fried tofu, prawn and flour fritters, chopped to order and
drenched in a bright orange-red gravy of mashed sweet potato, ground roasted peanuts, chilli
and tamarind. There is a version with yellow noodles added, sold as *mee rojak*.

Which produces one of the best findings in this whole exercise. **Penang mee jawa and Penang
mee rojak occupy the same flavour space** — sweet-potato-and-peanut gravy over yellow noodles
with fritters and egg — *reached from two different kitchens*, Chinese on one side and Indian
Muslim on the other, and they have almost certainly influenced each other on the same street
for a century. The distinguishing variables are the stock (pork or prawn versus halal),
whether the sauce is poured over or tossed through, and tomato paste versus tamarind and
chilli. No single source states the relationship. That gap is itself worth recording.

Two more mamak institutions, briefly. **Roti canai** is adapted from South Asian flatbread —
closest living relative the Malabar parotta — and does not exist as such in India; the
etymology is genuinely disputed between Chennai, Malay *canai* ("to knead"), and *chana*. And
**teh tarik**: the post-war account is that chai sellers could no longer afford good leaf and
turned to *sarabat*, the dust and fragments left from processing, which was astringent — so
they adopted the British milk-and-sugar practice using condensed milk to mask the bitterness,
and pulled the tea to aerate, cool and emulsify it. Plausible, widely repeated, and probably a
tidy simplification of a gradual process.

## How curry got into a Chinese wok

This is the single most important cross-community transfer in Penang food, and the mechanism
is not what people assume.

Start with the word. **Curry** is from Tamil **கறி** *kari*, meaning a spiced sauce or a
gravy preparation — not a dish name. The OED traces the English word to sixteenth-century
Tamil usage. But **pre-mixed curry *powder* is a British eighteenth-century invention** for
Western kitchens: first advertised commercially in London in 1784, standardised in **Madras
from the 1830s** by British civil servants commissioning reproducible blends from local spice
merchants, and commercialised further after 1857. Indian cooking blended spices fresh, per
dish.

That distinction is everything. A Hokkien hawker could not realistically maintain a Tamil
spice-roasting practice — the labour, the equipment and the knowledge are all wrong. But he
could buy a packet, by the *katty*, from a Chulia Street shop. **Commodified curry powder is
what made curry portable into a Chinese wok.** The curry leaf travelled the same route but as
a plant, propagated in Indian Penang backyards and adopted by Chinese and Malay cooks.

So Penang **curry mee** is a four-way fusion you can decompose cleanly:

| Component | Thread |
| --- | --- |
| Yellow alkaline noodle and bee hoon | Chinese (Hokkien) |
| Curry spice base | South Indian / Indian Muslim, via **British-commodified powder** |
| Coconut milk, sambal, belacan, daun kesum | Malay and Nyonya |
| Coagulated pig's blood, pork, lard | Chinese — and specifically *non*-halal |

That last row is the sting. A dish with Indian spice and Malay coconut is fixed permanently on
the Chinese side of the halal boundary by cubes of pig's blood. Fusion in Penang does not
dissolve boundaries; it decorates them.

There is an underappreciated general point here. Empire shows up in Penang street food not as
British *cuisine* — nobody is eating colonial food — but as a **supply chain**. Standardised
curry powder. Condensed and evaporated milk in a place with no dairy. Bottled tomato and chilli
sauce. Later, an instant noodle block. Every one of those is a datable node, and each one made
a dish possible that could not have existed the year before.

## The Chettiars, and the Indian food that is not in the noodle canon

The non-Muslim Tamil presence in Penang is largely a **labour** story. Over **1.7 million
Indians** were recruited into Malaya between roughly 1840 and 1942, first under indenture and
then under the *kangani* system — *kangani* being Tamil for headman or overseer, an experienced
worker sent home with cash advances to recruit kin and neighbours by leveraging village, caste
and kinship ties, a system that reliably slid into debt bondage. It dominated after 1910 when
rubber boomed and was abolished in 1938 when Indian emigration to Malaya was banned. Penang was
the primary northern port of entry and Province Wellesley one of the estate zones.

The **Nattukottai Chettiars** (Nagarathar) were the merchant-banker caste from Sivagangai and
Pudukottai — the name means "people with palatial houses in the countryside." They financed
much of the region's commerce, clustered on **Lebuh Penang**, and built the Thendayuthapani
temple on Waterfall Road in the 1850s — sources give c.1854 and vary, today the terminus of Penang's Thaipusam chariot
procession.

Their food footprint is the vegetarian South Indian one — banana leaf rice, idli, dosai, vadai,
rasam — plus the Chettinad spice tradition. It is a major part of eating in Penang and almost
entirely absent from the noodle canon, which is itself a finding worth stating rather than
glossing over.

One structural note before leaving it. **Putu mayam** is Tamil *idiyappam* — rice-flour dough
extruded through a sieve into vermicelli threads and steamed, eaten with grated coconut and
palm sugar. Achaya places idiyappam, appam, idli, dosa and vadai in the ancient Tamil country by
the first century CE on Sangam-literature evidence, and it travelled to Sri Lanka as string
hoppers, to Malaysia as putu mayam, to Indonesia as putu mayang. Which means **extrusion as a
noodle technology arrived in the peninsula from at least two directions** — Chinese and South
Indian — and that idiyappam, Malay putu mayang and Chinese *loh shi fun* occupy adjacent
niches without any of them descending from the others. Convergence, not diffusion.

## The Hadhramis, and the pilgrim port

Hadhrami Arabs, from the valley in what is now Yemen, had been trading in North Sumatran,
Malay, Kedah and Penang ports **since roughly the thirteenth century**, typically marrying
locally and settling; Sayyid lineages carried religious prestige that translated into political
and commercial leverage across the archipelago — the pattern scholarship calls the
"stranger-king" phenomenon.

Penang's Arab-Malay enclave is **Lebuh Acheh** and its surroundings, still the most legible
Malay-Muslim quarter in George Town. Two figures anchor it. **Tengku Syed Hussain Al-Aidid** —
Arab trader *and* member of the Acehnese royal house — moved to Penang in 1792, founded the
Acheen Street Mosque in 1808, and held jurisdiction under Islamic rather than colonial law
within his territory; he remade the character of the street until his death in 1840. And **Syed
Mohamed Alatas**, a wealthy Acehnese merchant of Arab descent and a pepper trader between
Penang and Aceh, who ran arms to the Acehnese resistance after the Dutch attack of the early
1870s. His house survives as the Syed Alatas Mansion.

**The single most food-relevant fact about Arab Penang is the hajj.** Before air travel,
Acheen Street was the centre of hajj organisation for the whole northern region. Pilgrims from
North Sumatra, southern Siam and the northern peninsula assembled there to arrange passage,
buy provisions, and wait — sometimes for weeks — for ships to Jeddah. Penang was, in effect, a
**pilgrim port**. That produced a concentration of Arabic-literate scholars, printers and
booksellers; a floating population of Muslims from across the region eating in the same lanes;
and a demand for portable, spice-heavy, halal, wheat-based food.

The lodging houses, *kedai kopi* and provision shops of the pilgrim trade are a plausible
transmission channel for the whole Arab-Indian spice-and-wheat register into Penang street
food. That inference is reasoned rather than sourced, and the graph flags it as
interpretation — but it is the best available explanation for why a Chinese-majority island
has such a developed halal wheat-food culture.

### Vocabulary, and one caution

- **Murtabak** ← Arabic *muṭabbaq*, "folded," describing the technique. The dominant account
  places its origin in Yemen — which had a substantial Indian population — spreading through
  Indian traders both to India and eastward, with an Abbasid recipe for *muṭbaq* in al-Baghdadi's
  *Kitab al-Tabikh*. A competing account has it created in Kerala and carried *to* Arabia. The
  Indonesian route is explicitly credited to Hadhrami traders on Java's coast. Direction of
  travel: disputed. The Arabic root: not.
- **Korma / kurma** ← Hindustani *qorma* ← Persian *qōrma* ← Turkic *qawirma*, "fried thing."
  Cognates in Persian *ghormeh* and Turkish *kavurma*. Beware the homonym: *kurma* also means
  date, the fruit, from a separate Arabic loan.
- **Biryani** ← Persian *birinj* (rice) + *biriyan* (to fry before cooking), introduced to
  Malaysia by both Indian Muslim and Arab diaspora.
- **Ghee**, wheat and *roti* culture, rose water, saffron and dried fruit — the Indo-Persian
  material signature in Penang Muslim cooking.
- **The sweet-spice quartet**: cinnamon, clove, star anise, cardamom, plus fennel, coriander
  and cumin.

And here is the caution, because it is tempting and it is wrong. That sweet-spice register
shows up most strikingly in *Chinese* dishes — Penang lor mee is defined by a five-spice aroma
with star anise and cinnamon driving it, and the whole *lor* braise family runs on the same
base. Do **not** read that as Arab influence. **Five-spice (五香粉) is Chinese and star anise is
a Chinese native.** Cassia, clove, cardamom and star anise circulated on the *same* Indian
Ocean routes for a millennium, so the warm-spice register in Penang has genuinely convergent
Chinese and Arab-Indian sources. The honest claim is convergence and mutual reinforcement, not
one-way influence, and the dataset flags any assertion otherwise.

Similarly on *sup kambing / sup tulang*: in Malaysia this is specifically **mamak** — thick,
oily, yellow-brown mutton broth heavy with coriander, fennel and cumin, and tied to *korban*
slaughter at Eid al-Adha. Notably, in **Indonesia** the same dish is attributed to Arab
migration of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries rather than to Indian Muslims. Penang's
version therefore sits at exactly the confluence: Arab spice logic arriving through an Indian
Muslim kitchen. Which is a fair summary of this whole chapter.
