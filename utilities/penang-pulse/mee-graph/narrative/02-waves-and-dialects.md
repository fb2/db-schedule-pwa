# 2. Waves: how the Chinese arrived, and which noodles came with them

"Chinese food in Penang" is not a category that predicts anything. "Teochew food in Penang"
predicts a great deal — a clear broth, a rice noodle, braised duck. The unit of analysis
here is the dialect group, and the dialect group is really shorthand for *a home prefecture,
an arrival date, and an occupational niche*. Get those three right and the food falls out
almost deterministically.

## The shape of the migration

Chinese contact with the Straits long predates the British, but the movement that made
Penang runs from 1786 to 1949 and has a clear structure.

**1786–1830: foundation.** James Low's 1836 list of Chinese in Penang already names
Hokkien, Zhangzhou, "Kainchoo" (Jiaying Hakka), Teochew, "Macao" and several Cantonese
sub-districts. Every dialect group in this graph was present within fifty years of
founding. The institutions arrive early too: the Cantonese carpenters' guild **Loo Pun
Hong** on Penang Street in 1801, the earliest Chinese medicine shop in 1796, and around 1800
**Kong Hock Keong** (廣福宮 — literally "Guangdong–Fujian Temple") on Pitt Street, founded
jointly by Hokkiens and Cantonese and functioning as the community's neutral tribunal. The
fact that they needed a neutral tribunal in 1800 is itself the story.

**1840s–1930s: the *sinkeh*.** 新客, "new guest" — overwhelmingly coolies and manual
labourers, arriving as sojourners intending to make money in the Nanyang and go home. Most
could not pay their passage, so the mechanism was the credit-ticket system: a broker or
employer advanced the fare and controlled the contract, the transport and the deployment.
Abuses reached notorious levels by the 1850s; regulation arrived in 1877, roughly thirty
years late, with the Chinese Immigrants Ordinance and the Chinese Protectorate. Over a
million Chinese migrated to British Malaya between the 1870s and the 1930s.

Against the *sinkeh* stood the *laukeh* — 老客, "old guest" — the locally rooted,
often English-educated, multi-generational Chinese, which in the Straits Settlements
overlaps almost entirely with the Peranakan. This was never a hard boundary in Penang;
intermarriage between Peranakans and *sinkeh* was common. And that is exactly why Penang
Hokkien food is not "Fujian food in Malaysia." It is a creolised register. Tamarind, torch
ginger, belacan, sambal and *hae ko* entered the Hokkien kitchen through the *laukeh* side
of the family.

**What made Penang boom:** four structural shifts, all external. The Suez Canal moved the
main trade route from the Sunda Strait to the Straits of Malacca. British intervention in the
Malay states intensified tin extraction. The Dutch opened Sumatra's east-coast plantation
belt. And rubber arrived. Penang was a port of *entry* as much as a destination — as Kuchler
puts it, every pioneer opening the tin deposits of southern Thailand, Larut and Kinta was
based here.

**1930s–1949: the door closes.** A quota system arrived with the Aliens (Immigration
Restriction) Ordinance of 1930; the **Aliens Ordinance of 1932**, in force from 1 April 1933,
capped adult Chinese *male* arrivals while actively encouraging Chinese *women* to enter, to
correct a grossly male-skewed sex ratio. The food-history consequence is
inferential but strong: the 1930s female influx is the demographic precondition for
family-run hawker stalls, and for home cooking crossing into commercial street food. A
bachelor society eats out; a family society sells food.

Then the Occupation, and then the Emergency. An ordinance in July 1949 effectively closed
the pipeline that had run since 1786; by 1952 citizenship extended to most Malaya-born with
a Malaya-born parent, and by 1957 **81% of Penang's Chinese population was locally born**.

That 1949 hinge is the most consequential date in this entire graph, and it is not about food
at all. After it, no fresh cohort arrived from China to correct or refresh the dialect
repertoires. Everything since is endogenous local evolution. It is precisely why Penang, KL
and Singapore versions of "the same" dish have drifted so far apart — they stopped being
corrected against a common source at the same moment, and then had seventy-five years to
diverge.

## The numbers, and a warning about them

Penang, 1957 census, State totals — 299,679 Chinese:

| Group | 1957 | ~2000 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hokkien | 113,943 (38.0%) | 54.2% |
| Cantonese | 49,483 (16.5%) | 11.7% |
| Teochew | 29,475 (9.8%) | 22.3% |
| Hakka | 21,988 (7.3%) | 7.2% |
| Hainanese | 8,436 (2.8%) | 2.0% |
| Foochow / Hokchia | 3,638 (1.2%) | 0.6% |
| Henghua | 378 | — |

Two warnings. First, the Cantonese line moves a great deal between datasets, and Kuchler
flags why: **Cantonese organised by place of origin (*hui kuan*) while Hokkiens organised by
surname (*kongsi*)**. Any dataset built on association records will overstate Cantonese
numbers and understate Hokkien. Second, "province is not dialect." The president of Penang's
own Kwangtung & Tengchow Association makes the point bluntly: Guangdong-origin migrants
included Cantonese, Hakka, Teochew and Hainanese speakers, and Hakka bodies from Tengchow
and *Yongding — both in Fujian* — joined the Guangdong umbrella purely on the basis of shared
speech. Speech, not geography, was the organising fact.

For comparison, the same census logic elsewhere: Perak is Cantonese 32.6% / Hokkien 24.9%;
Kuala Lumpur is roughly Cantonese and Hokkien at parity; **Sabah is Hakka 58%**; **Sarawak
is Foochow 34.8%** and Hakka 31.5%; Kedah is Hokkien 45.1% / Teochew 27.4%. That table is
the backbone of any argument about which town's noodles belong to whom, and it explains at a
glance why pan mee is a Klang Valley dish, why Ipoh cooks a Cantonese repertoire, and why a
stall in Penang selling "Sabah pan mee" is invoking a genuinely Hakka noodle culture from a
thousand kilometres away.

Inside Penang in 1957, the geography was equally specific: three-quarters of George Town's
Chinese were Hokkien and Cantonese; **86% of all Penang Cantonese lived in George Town**;
**40% of Teochews lived rurally**; **60% of Hakkas lived in the countryside**; fishing
settlements were almost exclusively Hokkien; and nearly all vegetable farmers on the Penang
Hill slopes were Hakka.

## Hokkien 福建

**Home:** southern Fujian — the low valleys of the Jiulong and Jin rivers, meaning Zhangzhou,
Quanzhou and Xiamen, plus Anxi, Hui'an and Haicheng. Penang Hokkien specifically derives from
a *Zhangzhou* sub-dialect. All seaports. That matters: this is a seafood cuisine because the
people came from harbours.

**Arrival:** earliest and continuous, from 1786.

**Niche:** everything, by weight of numbers, but with a coastal bias toward shipping and
fishing. Vaughan in 1854: "Fuk-kien men are tailors, shopkeepers, merchants and owners of
spice-plantations." Beach Street was the Hokkien–Teochew wholesale spine. Trishaw pullers
were largely Hokkiens from Hui'an living around Magazine Road. And at the top, the Big Five
clans — **Khoo, Cheah, Yeoh, Lim, Tan**, the *Goh Tai Seh* — ran regional trade, shipping,
cash crops, tin and the opium revenue farms.

The clan geography is worth dwelling on, because it explains how food trades got their
territory. Leong San Tong Khoo Kongsi, founded 1835, temple completed 1906, sits at Cannon
Square; nearly every house in the surrounding street block is Khoo Kongsi property, with
"Sin Kang" signboards naming the ancestral village. The Cheahs held Armenian Street and Toh
Aka Lane. The Lees from Te Suah village concentrated along Weld Quay, mostly as sampan men.
Two adjacent stilt villages in the southern harbour were occupied respectively *entirely* by
Lim-surname Hokkiens and *entirely* by Chew-clan Hokkiens — distinguishable on a map by
which way the gables ran. Surname endogamy expressed as built form.

A surname kongsi owning a whole street block means the shophouses, and therefore the
ground-floor food businesses and the licensed hawker pitches in front of them, sat inside a
single lineage's patronage network. There were 81 surname associations, 57 regional
associations and 71 occupational associations in Penang in 1963. Nobody wrote a recipe down;
they controlled who got a pitch.

**Noodles:** the prawn-head broth of Xiamen, which becomes Penang Hokkien mee. The *lor*
braise of Zhangzhou, which becomes lor mee. The salted wheat thread *mee sua* of Quanzhou,
and its collapsed-porridge form 麵線糊, which survives in Penang as mee suah koh. Plus the
yellow alkaline noodle itself, which the Hokkien noodle factories made and sold to
absolutely everybody.

**And the naming.** Because Hokkien was the lingua franca of the Penang street, *every*
hawker dish acquired a Hokkien name regardless of who cooked it first. Char kway teow is a
Teochew dish with a Hokkien-pronounced name. This is not a mistranslation — Teochew and
Hokkien are both Southern Min and *kóe-tiâu* is comprehensible in either — it is a
prestige effect, and it has misled a century of food writing.

## Teochew 潮州

**Home:** the lower Han River valley of eastern Guangdong — Chaozhou and Shantou (Swatow), a
fruit-growing region. Most Penang Teochews came from small villages there. The Teochew
Association at 127 Chulia Street, founded 1855, still holds 2,316 registration records from
1919, each listing the registrant's district of origin in Chaoshan. That is probably the
richest unexploited primary source in Penang food history.

**Niche:** agriculture and cash crops above all. Teochews established the gambier and pepper
industries; they managed the sugar plantations at Perai after 1800; cash crops drew Teochew
planters to Bukit Mertajam, Bukit Tambun and Batu Kawan. In Kedah and north Perak they were
predominantly fishermen; elsewhere grocers and petty traders. In town, the enclave was
around Carnavon and Chulia Streets, with a Teochew cluster on Kimberley Street still called
*Swatow Kay*.

**Noodles:** the flat rice noodle 粿條 and everything made from it. Koay teow th'ng, char
kway teow, char koay kak, koay chiap. The *lou* master-stock braise and, with it, braised
duck — duck is the Teochew meat of choice, and Penang's Teochew hawkers built essentially
their whole repertoire around it. The clear broth as an aesthetic commitment: preserve the
original flavour of the ingredient.

And the reach of it. Koay teow th'ng, Vietnam's *hủ tiếu*, Cambodia's *kuy teav* and Thai
*ก๋วยเตี๋ยว kuaitiao* are the same two Teochew syllables. Thai *บะหมี่ bamee* is a loan of
Teochew *bah-mī*. **The Thai words for rice noodle and egg noodle are Chinese dialect
words** — which settles the direction of transmission and makes the Teochew diaspora legible
as a distribution network rather than a set of isolated communities.

**One caveat, and it is a large one.** It is repeated everywhere, Michelin included, that
the Teochews dominate Penang's street food trade despite Hokkien numerical dominance. This
is the load-bearing claim of the whole "Penang food is Teochew food with Hokkien names"
thesis, and no scholarly source establishes it. In this graph it is tagged `medium`
confidence with a flag, and it is the single most important open question in the dataset.

## Cantonese 廣府

**Home:** the Pearl River and Xi Jiang deltas — Guangzhou and its satellite districts,
including the Sze Yup counties. Kuchler notes drily that these are the same districts that
supplied most Chinese emigration to America.

**Niche:** the artisan and craft trades. Vaughan in 1854: "All the carpenters, blacksmiths,
shoemakers and other laborious tradesmen are of the first... they are excellent squatters
and may be called pioneers to the Chin-chews." Kuchler's own 1962–63 survey found
goldsmiths, gold and silver merchants, barbers, carpenters, masons and photographers still
predominantly Cantonese — with **36 of 90 shophouses within a hundred yards of Campbell
Street and Rope Walk occupied by silver- and goldsmiths**, and over a thousand goldsmiths
estimated in George Town by 1954, a free-port effect. The food footprint is the dim sum
shops on Cintra Street.

**Noodles:** wonton noodles from Guangzhou and the thin round egg noodle they ride on;
*yi mein* from Yi Bingshou's Huizhou kitchen; the flat rice noodle under its Cantonese name
河粉 and therefore Ipoh's kai si hor fun; chee cheong fun; and *wat tan hor*, the silky-egg
gravy technique.

In Penang the Cantonese are a minority on a Hokkien island, and their dishes read as
slightly imported — wantan mee and chee cheong fun are unmistakably Penang food, but they
are Penang's Cantonese food. Outside Penang the picture inverts: the Cantonese were the
largest group in the Federated Malay States in 1901, and Ipoh is a Cantonese and Hakka town
built by tin.

## Hakka 客家

**Home:** uniquely among the five, entirely **inland** — the upper valleys of the Dong, Mei
and Ting rivers in the Guangdong–Fujian–Jiangxi border uplands. Jiaying (modern Meixian),
Dabu, Huizhou, Yongding, Zengcheng, Hepo. And the sub-group destinations were remarkably
specific: Singapore and Melaka Hakkas were largely Dabu; Johor Hakkas Hepo; the KL and Larut
mine workers Huizhou and Jiaying; Kuching Huizhou, Bau Lufeng, Serian Jiaying.

Being inland is the whole culinary story. Hakka food is wheat dough and preserved meat, not
seafood, because that is what the uplands gave them.

**Niche:** mining first — in 1891, 64% of the 28,125 Chinese in Selangor's tin mines were
Hakka; by 1911 Hakkas were 46% of Selangor's Chinese. Hakka women did not practise
foot-binding and worked in large numbers as **dulang washers**, panning ore standing in
water, a role no other dialect group's women filled. Then Chinese medicine halls, textiles
and pawnbroking. In Penang specifically: medicine halls and pawn shops in town, and the
vegetable farms and rubber smallholdings of Balik Pulau and the Penang Hill slopes.

**Noodles:** the hand-cut or hand-torn wheat dough — 板麵, *pan mee* — and its Malaysian
descendants; Hakka mee from Dabu; and, transplanted to Borneo, Sarawak's kolo mee and
Sabah's Tuaran mee and *sang nyuk mee*.

Penang is the *weakest* Malaysian site for Hakka noodle culture — around 7% of the Chinese
population, rurally concentrated. Sabah, Ampang and Seremban are the strong ones. Which is
why pan mee in Penang is a late-twentieth-century import rather than a heritage dish, and
why calling it "Sabah pan mee" is a piece of commercial borrowing rather than a provenance
claim.

## Hainanese 海南

**Home:** Hainan island. Penang's Hainanese came overwhelmingly from **Haikou and Qiongshan**,
with a smaller Wenchang group — a correction to the usual "Wenchang/Qionghai" formulation.

**Arrival: late.** This is the pivotal fact in the whole chapter.

By the time the Hainanese arrived in numbers, the trades were locked up: commerce was
Hokkien, crafts were Cantonese, mining was Hakka, agriculture was Teochew. So they took what
was left — houseboys, domestic servants, ships' crew, and **cooks for British colonial and
Straits Chinese households**. Clement Liang of the Penang Heritage Trust puts it in one
sentence: "The Hainanese arrived later than the other groups so they ended up mostly as cooks
for European and wealthy Chinese households."

Then, from the late 1930s, they monetised it: hotels, restaurants and above all the
**kopitiam**. Kuchler's occupational survey confirms it — "Hainanese and Hokchiu specialise
in hotels and restaurants." Penang's examples include the Western-food restaurants Wing
Look, Sin Kean Aun and Hollywood, and Loke Thye Kee on Burmah Road, founded by two Hainanese
brothers in 1919.

This is why the smallest dialect group — 8,436 people in Penang in 1957 — produced Malaysia's
entire colonial-fusion cuisine. Hainanese chicken rice, chicken chop and "Western food,"
*mee Hailam*, and the wok-roasted *kopi* tradition all exist because the Hainanese were the
only dialect group whose economic niche literally *was* cooking, and cooking for non-Chinese
employers at that. Their contribution to noodles is adaptive rather than ancestral: mee
Hailam is a Malayan invention by Hainanese cooks, not a transplant from Hainan.

And it gives us one of the most important transmission mechanisms in the graph. Singapore's
Katong laksa reached the street because Hainanese domestic workers learned the household
laksa of their Peranakan employers and, when jobs were scarce after 1945, took it out and
sold it. As Tony Boey observes, no Peranakan family would have permitted an heirloom laksa
recipe to be peddled as street food. The cook was the leak.

## Foochow 福州 and Henghua 興化

**Foochow (Hokchiu)** are from Mindong, eastern Fujian, and their Malaysian history is
unusual because it was *engineered* rather than chain-driven. In 1901 **Wong Nai Siong**, a
Methodist, negotiated with Rajah Charles Brooke to establish an agricultural settlement and
landed 72 Fuzhou pioneers at Sibu, which they called *Sin Hockchew*, New Foochow; over a
thousand Christian Foochows were there by 1903. In 1903 the government contracted the
Methodist Episcopal Mission to plant a Foochow colony at **Sitiawan, Perak**, explicitly to
feed the growing coolie population. Malaysia's noodle geography was, in this one case,
agricultural policy.

Their signature is fish balls stuffed with minced pork, red glutinous rice wine, *kampua
mee*, and hand-pulled *mee sua* — Sitiawan and Sarawak are the country's two mee sua centres.
Which produces one of this dataset's most persistent corrections: **Sarawak laksa and kolo
mee are Kuching dishes, not Foochow ones.** Kuching is Hakka, Hokkien and Teochew. The
Foochow noodle is kampua, from Sibu. Every source that assigns kolo mee to the Foochow is
conflating two towns three hundred kilometres apart.

**Henghua** — 378 people in Penang in 1957 — are Puxian Min speakers from Putian, neither
Hokkien nor Foochow, and their finer, silkier bee hoon is their calling card. Fried Heng Hwa
bee hoon is a named dish across the Straits. A group most dialect maps omit entirely, with
one noodle to its name and that noodle recognisably its own.

## What the tin wars have to do with lunch

One last note, because it recurs in every popular account and is more slippery than it
looks. Tin organised Chinese labour by dialect and sub-dialect, and those organisations
fought. The First Larut War broke out in July 1861 over control of a watercourse serving the
mines. The 1867 Penang Riots ran ten days — Gee Hin with some twenty thousand members
against Toh Peh Kong with nine, the latter led by **Khoo Thean Teik**, of one of the Big Five
Hokkien clans, which is the direct link between respectable clan power and street power. The
trigger was a rambutan skin thrown during an argument. Over a hundred people died; each
faction paid a five-thousand-dollar "voluntary penalty"; the Suppression of Dangerous
Societies Ordinance followed in 1869.

The temptation is to map societies neatly onto dialects. Do not. Sources give three mutually
contradictory alignments for 1867, and the Penang alignment differed from the Perak one. What
*is* defensible: the societies were dialect-inflected but not dialect-pure, membership
shifted over decades, and the granular evidence is about property rather than allegiance — of
80 to 90 shophouses in Larut in 1865, 70 to 80 were owned by Haishan Hakkas, 11 by Huizhou
Hakkas, and 2 by Hainanese.

The useful residue for food history is spatial. Secret society halls sit in the oldest part
of George Town; surname kongsi houses sit in the later-developed southern quarter — because
in the first decades after 1786 the societies were already organised while the clans
consolidated more slowly, building their halls only from around 1830. Walk the streets in
that order and you are walking the sequence in which Chinese Penang organised itself, which
is also the sequence in which it divided up the right to sell food.
