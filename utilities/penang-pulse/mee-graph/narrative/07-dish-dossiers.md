# 7. Dish dossiers

Sixteen bowls tasted, in series order, then the ones still on the list. Each dossier gives the
names, the noodle, what each cultural thread actually contributed, and what is contested — with
confidence stated rather than implied.

---

# Part one: the sixteen tasted

## 1 · Jawa Mee — 33 Best Food Hub, Tanjung Tokong · 20 Jul 2026

*Mee Jawa / mi Jawa. Sold at some stalls as mee rojak.*

**Noodle:** blanched yellow alkaline wheat.

**The bowl:** a thick, sweet-savoury gravy of boiled sweet potato, tomato paste and shrimp
stock — or pork-rib stock at Chinese stalls, which fixes it as non-halal. Bean sprouts, boiled
potato, hard-boiled egg, sliced fried beancurd, prawn fritters, shredded lettuce, fried
shallots, ground peanuts, crisp fritter shards. Lime and a blob of sambal.

**Threads:** Chinese noodle and stock; Javanese sauce logic — the *pecel* and *petis* family of
peanut-and-sweet-potato sauces; Malay souring and sambal; and, arriving from the other side of
the street, the Indian-Muslim pasembur gravy it shares a flavour space with.

**Contested — genuinely.** Three accounts circulate: Chinese-Javanese Peranakans migrating from
Medan and Malacca developed it in Penang; direct Javanese or Minangkabau settlers introduced it;
or it is a local Penang Chinese invention wearing an exotic name. *Malay Mail*'s assessment is
the honest default and this dataset adopts it — the origin is unclear, the name points at the
Java Peranakan heyday, and the ingredients point at several cultures at once. **Confidence:
low.**

**Two things worth knowing.** First, Penang mee jawa and Indonesian *mie jawa* are **false
cognates**: the Javanese dish is a charcoal-cooked chicken noodle in clear broth, nothing like
this. Second, the Penang Chinese version uses little or no turmeric, fennel, curry powder or
rempah, and notably **omits taucu** — dropping the Chinese-origin ingredient precisely because
the Malay mee rebus had already made it its signature. Penangites call this "the sweet Jawa mee."
NLB adds the unromantic corroboration: the gravy is flavoured with a can of tomato soup.

## 2 · "Sabah Pan Mee" (soup) — Pulau Tikus · Jul 2026

*Pan mee / ban mian 板麵. Also mee hoon kueh 麵粉粿, and Hakka dao-ma-chet 刀嬤切.*

**Noodle:** fresh hand-formed **non-alkaline** wheat dough — the only major noodle in this graph
without alkali in it. Three forms are sold at one stall and Malaysians treat them as one family:
hand-torn irregular pieces (mee hoon kueh, the Hokkien method), flat cut strips about a
centimetre wide, and near-round spaghetti-gauge strands sold as "you mee."

**The bowl:** dried-anchovy stock with crisp *ikan bilis* garnish, minced pork, dried shiitake,
sweet potato leaves or *sayur manis*, a poached egg. Sambal on the side; vinegar to balance.

**Threads:** Hakka dough tradition from the Guangdong uplands, Hokkien hand-tearing method, and
a **Malay-archipelago anchovy stock** that is the single clearest marker that pan mee is a
Malaysian dish rather than a transplanted Chinese one. Mainland ban mian uses pork or plain
water.

**The name is the interesting part.** No dish called "Sabah pan mee" is documented in Sabah.
Sabah's real items are *sang nyuk mee* — reported invented in Tawau in 1979 — and the
district-noodle set: Tuaran, Beaufort, Tenom, Tamparuli, Kota Belud. What is almost certainly
being sold under this name is ordinary pan mee made with **sayur manis** rather than sweet potato
leaves, borrowing the reputation of a genuinely distinctive Hakka noodle culture from across the
South China Sea. Sabah is the one Malaysian state with a **Hakka-majority** Chinese population —
58% by 2000 — and organises its noodles by *district* rather than by dialect, which is why the
appeal to Sabah carries weight even when it does not carry a dish. **Confidence: medium** that
the name denotes nothing at home.

**One thing food writing never mentions.** *Sauropus androgynus* — sayur manis, mani cai, sold
in the peninsula as "Sabah vegetable" — carries a documented toxicity risk. An epidemic of
**bronchiolitis obliterans**, irreversible obstructive lung disease, occurred in Taiwan in
1994–95 among people consuming large quantities raw or juiced. It must be cooked, and should not
be eaten raw in quantity. This is in the medical literature, unlike almost everything else in
this chapter. **Confidence: high.**

## 3 · Kolo Mee — Hock Beng 88 · Jul 2026

*乾撈麵 / 哥羅麵. Malay mi kolok. Iban mi kering, mi rangkai.*

**Noodle:** thin springy egg-and-alkali wheat noodle.

**The bowl:** blanched, shocked, and tossed in rendered pork fat and crisp lard, shallot oil and
fried shallots, and light soy; topped with minced pork, sliced char siu, spring onion, pickled
green chilli, with a bowl of clear soup on the side. Three canonical styles — white or plain,
**red** with char siu marinade tossed through, **black** with dark soy.

**Threads:** Hakka in the recipe, Cantonese in the char siu, and — in the halal version — Iban
and Malay Sarawakian in the naming.

**The genealogy, which is usually got wrong.** Kolo mee derives from Hakka **Tai Pu / *yan mee***
("salted noodles") from **Dabu county, Meizhou**, Guangdong. The credited pioneer is **Kiew Shao
Nyap**, a native of Baihou in Dabu, who reached Kuching in the 1920s and sold his hometown dish
from a street stall. That is unusually good provenance — a named person, a named village, a named
decade, and a named parent dish. **Confidence: medium.**

Sources that assign kolo mee to the **Foochow** are conflating **Kuching** — which is Hakka,
Hokkien and Teochew — with **Sibu**, which is Foochow and whose noodle is *kampua mee*. The same
conflation afflicts Sarawak laksa. Both towns are in Sarawak and three hundred kilometres apart.

**Why it matters more than it looks.** Kolo mee is one of the very few Chinese-origin pork dishes
in this dataset to have generated a **fully accepted halal parallel with its own
indigenous-language names**. Not a compromise version, not a substitute — a dish Iban and Malay
Sarawakians eat as their own. Gazetted as a Malaysian heritage food in 2024.

**And its relationship to wantan mee.** Same *idea* — thin egg noodle tossed dry in fat and soy,
char siu on top, soup on the side — reached from two different Chinese traditions. Kon lo wantan
mee is Cantonese and defined by caramelised dark soy and by the wonton as its reason for
existing; kolo mee is Hakka, defined by lard and shallot oil in its classic white form, with no
wontons and minced pork as default. Convergent siblings with a Cantonese borrowing edge, not
parent and child.

## 4 · Lam Mee — Hock Beng 88 · 22 Jul 2026

*淋麵 (attribution disputed). Also seh jit mee 生日麵, Nyonya lam mee, birthday mee.*

**Noodle:** yellow alkaline wheat, sometimes with bee hoon mixed in.

**The bowl:** a light, clear-but-rich broth from prawns plus pork ribs or chicken carcass,
simmered for hours and **poured over the assembled noodles at serving**. Finely shredded
toppings arranged on top: omelette, chicken, prawns, blanched bean sprouts, long beans, fried
shallots. **Sambal belacan and calamansi on the side**, without which Penangites will tell you it
is not lam mee.

**Etymology.** If 淋麵, then 淋 is "to pour liquid over" — *drenched noodles*, named for a
gesture, which is unusual and rather lovely. 燜麵, "to braise covered," is also seen and fits the
preparation less well.

**Ritual.** A birthday and longevity dish for milestone years — sixtieth, seventieth, eightieth —
prepared as an offering for prayers and distributed to relatives, traditionally with a large red
*ang ku kueh*. Long unbroken noodles mean long life; slurp, never bite through. Penang is
distinctive in having elevated *lam mee* into the birthday role, where most Hokkien households
use *mee sua*. The dataset models these as two branches of one `x-longevity-noodle` concept.

**What it did to its inheritance.** The account Penang cooks give: Baba-Nyonya descendants of
Fujian immigrants brought **lor mee** and adjusted the broth and toppings to Nyonya taste until
it branched off. If right, lam mee kept lor mee's *structure* — noodle, poured liquid, assembled
toppings — and **discarded its defining *lor* braise**, replacing dark soy and cornstarch with
clear prawn stock. A creole dish is often not a blend but a substitution: keep the architecture,
swap out the load-bearing element. **Confidence: low** (family-tradition history).

**A claim to reject.** That lam mee *is* loh mee, lightened for Cantonese palates and
re-pronounced. It fails on the broth (clear versus thick starch — different in kind, not degree),
on the ritual function loh mee lacks, on geography (Penang is not Cantonese-majority), and on
etymology (淋 works perfectly well on its own). Carried in the dataset as `confused_with` and as
an explicit `unevidenced_link`.

**Also:** far more a home and celebration dish than a hawker one. Hawker versions are widely
described as different from and inferior to home-cooked. Worth knowing before judging a bowl of
it.

## 5 · Hokkien / Prawn Mee — Restoran 77 Food Yard · 23 Jul 2026

*福建麵 in Penang, but ordered by locals as **hae mee / hē-mī 蝦麵**. Elsewhere Penang prawn
noodle 檳城蝦麵. Malay mi udang.*

**Noodle:** a **mixture** of thick yellow alkaline wheat and thin bee hoon, in whatever ratio you
ask for — "mee," "mee hoon," or "chap" for mixed. The mixed-noodle option is itself a Straits
innovation and a diagnostic of the region.

**The bowl:** broth of prawn heads and shells **dry-fried first** (爆香, to caramelise and release
the crustacean flavour) then boiled for hours with pork bones and often dried shrimp. Blanched
noodles, kangkung, bean sprouts, halved hard-boiled egg, sliced pork, prawns, fried shallots,
lard, fish cake. Optional add-ons run to pork skin, ribs, intestine, stewed knuckle, roast belly.

**The ancestor, and why it settles an argument.** **Xiamen prawn noodle 廈門蝦麵** still exists —
built on fried prawn heads and pork bones, with prawns and pork slices, optionally intestine,
prawn balls, char siu and duck blood. It is unspiced, garnished with coriander and raw minced
garlic, and treated as a *seasonal summer* dish, hence the pun 夏麵, since 廈 (Xia, Amoy) and 夏
(xià, summer) are homophones and the prawns are best in summer. **Confidence: high** — this is
one of the best-supported claims in the whole dataset.

Which kills the popular story that Penang Hokkien mee was invented in wartime scarcity from
prawn-head scraps. The parent dish survives in Fujian, and an eyewitness account of occupied
Penang says there were essentially **no noodles and hardly any rice** in those years, with
sustenance from tapioca and cassava.

**The two substitutions that make it Penang's.** *Kangkung replaces coriander.* And **sambal
belacan is cooked into the stock** where Xiamen uses raw minced garlic for heat and no sambal at
all — extra sambal still arrives on the side. That is the cleanest documented case of a Fujian
dish creolising in Penang, and it is worth noticing that both changes are *Malay* ingredients
entering through the Peranakan side of the family.

**And the naming disaster.** Three unrelated dishes are called Hokkien mee — this soup, KL's
dark braise-fried noodle, and Singapore's pale wet fry. Penangites resolve it by calling the
local fried dish "Hokkien char"; KL calls this one "prawn mee." One ethnonym, three dishes, zero
relationship beyond the makers' shared ancestry. Wikipedia's separate claim that *all* Hokkien
mee variants descend from lor mee is uncited and conflates two different Fujianese traditions;
the dataset carries it as an `unevidenced_link`.

## 6 · Mee Suah Koh — 33 Best Food Hub · 23 Jul 2026

*麵線糊 mī-sòaⁿ-kô͘. Locally also mee suah tau. In Quanzhou and Amoy, misua gor.*

**Noodle:** **mee sua** 麵線 — extremely thin salted wheat thread, hand-pulled and sun-dried.
麵 noodle + 線 **thread**. The salt is a processing aid that makes the dough extensible, not a
seasoning choice. It cooks in seconds and disintegrates if overcooked, which is the entire point
of the 糊 form: 糊 *kô͘* means paste, pulp, congee. *Misua pulp* — the noodle cooked until it
collapses.

**The bowl:** a sticky, gluey vermicelli soup, often topped with shredded crab meat. Sold in only
a handful of Penang coffee shops.

**Why this is the most interesting bowl on the tried list.** It is a **low-frequency survival**.
In Xiamen and Quanzhou today, 面线糊 is mainstream breakfast street food with a huge array of
add-ins — offal, oyster, fried fritter. In Penang it survives as a niche, preserving the
ancestral Quanzhou form **almost unchanged** while its more adaptable siblings, Hokkien mee and
lor mee, creolised heavily.

The lesson generalises. Adaptation and survival are not the same thing, and in Penang they often
run in opposite directions: the dishes that changed most are the ones that thrived, and the ones
that stayed closest to source are the ones you now have to hunt for. Explicitly described in the
literature as "a traditional Hokkien meal that Chinese immigrants brought more than a century
ago" and as *not* common hawker food. **Confidence: medium.**

**The sibling thread.** The same noodle carries the whole Hokkien ritual repertoire — birthdays
with two red-dyed eggs, post-partum confinement in ginger-and-sesame-oil broth, Lunar New Year —
and the Foochow *ang jiu* version in red glutinous-rice-wine chicken soup, standard for
birthdays, confinement and New Year in Sitiawan and Sarawak. One noodle, doing everyday work and
ceremonial work in the same city.

## 7 · Char Kway Teow — Restoran 77 Food Yard · 24 Jul 2026

*炒粿條, chhá-kóe-tiâu. Malay standardised as kuetiau goreng (DBP, 2021).*

**Noodle:** flat rice noodle about a centimetre wide. **Penang cuts thinner ribbons than
Singapore** and does not mix in yellow wheat noodle. Penang also fries a *lo shi fun* version
Singapore does not have.

**Etymology, word by word.** 炒 stir-fry, 粿 a steamed rice-flour cake, 條 a long narrow strip.
Literally "**stir-fried rice-cake strips**" — which is exactly what a flat rice noodle is: a
steamed rice sheet cut into ribbons. The Cantonese 河粉 "river flour," after Shahe in Guangzhou,
names the same object from a different angle. And there is a delightful footnote: the dish name
travelled *back into China* as a loanword, written 炒貴刁, characters chosen purely for sound and
meaning nothing — "fried expensive-cunning" — and menu-translated in some American Chinese
restaurants as "Fried Good Dale."

**The bowl:** very high heat for *wok hei*; lard and crisp lard croutons; garlic; light and dark
soy; chilli paste; whole prawns; **blood cockles**; Chinese chives; *lup cheong*; bean sprouts;
egg. Penang signatures: **duck egg** rather than chicken — richer, more custardy — and a banana
leaf on the plate, or a conical banana-leaf wrap with grass string for takeaway.

**Penang against Singapore.** Penang is **lighter and less sweet** because the sweet dark soy is
omitted or minimised. Singapore's is sweeter, wetter, darker, and bulked with yellow noodles,
reflecting a Hokkien majority's taste. Singapore switched to chicken eggs in the 1950s–60s as
duck farming ceased; Penang kept the duck egg. Singapore hawkers were pushed off lard by the
1999 pig virus epidemic and a 2006 health campaign; Penang was not.

**Name against ethnicity.** The name is Hokkien; the dish is Teochew, with Chaozhou the presumed
point of origin. That mismatch is the historically interesting datum rather than an error —
Hokkien was the lingua franca of the Penang street, and since Teochew and Hokkien are both
Southern Min, *kóe-tiâu* is comprehensible in either. Less a mistranslation than a prestige
effect.

**The origin story, and its collapse.** The universally repeated account — fishermen, farmers and
cockle-gatherers doubling as evening hawkers, the high fat content making it a cheap source of
energy — is on Wikipedia with two footnotes. The first points at Singapore NLB's Infopedia
article, which **does not contain the claim**. The second points at a **2016 newspaper
health-scare piece**. Plausible, and unsourced. **Confidence: low, borderline
fabricated-by-citation-drift** — and it is the single most-repeated unverified claim in Malaysian
and Singaporean food writing.

What *is* documented, and better: during the Occupation, tapioca-based noodles were used because
tapioca was cheap and the dish was fried in red palm oil, so wartime CKT was **red**. And in the
1950s a bean-sprout growers' strike over an order to use tap water instead of well water made
sprouts scarce, so **cai xin** was introduced as a substitute and stuck. Both sourced to National
Archives of Singapore oral history — unusually good evidence for a food claim.

**One local variant worth chasing.** Bukit Mertajam has a wetter, gravy-finished **char koay teow
basah**, locally associated with the **Malay** community. Poorly documented, and a strong
candidate for the mainland episode slot.

## 8 · Wantan Mee (soup) — Cheah Yew Market · 27 Jul 2026

*雲吞麵. Dry version kon lo mee 乾撈麵.*

**Noodle:** thin springy **egg noodle**, 幼麵 — wheat, egg and alkali, made in coils, low
hydration, traditionally bamboo-pole pressed. This is *the* Cantonese noodle in the Penang graph
and it is not interchangeable with Hokkien yellow noodle. It is also the same physical noodle as
Teochew *mee kia*, under a different dialect's name — which is the root of the "you mee" confusion.

**Etymology.** 雲吞 *wan tan* is a Cantonese phonetic rendering of 餛飩 *húntún*, and the
characters chosen mean "**swallowing clouds**" — a poetic re-spelling for the way the dumplings
float. 乾撈 is dry-tossed, the same 撈 as in *lo hei*.

**The bowl:** wontons; red-glazed **char siu**; choy sum; pickled green chilli in vinegar; fried
shallots or lard. The dry version's sauce is caramelised dark soy, light soy, sesame oil and
lard, with soup and more wontons alongside.

**The three-way divergence, which is the real story.** Guangzhou serves noodles and wontons
together **in hot soup** with vegetables. Hong Kong's canonical form is a small bowl of noodles
laid *on top of* prawn-forward wontons in a clear, intense dried-flounder-and-prawn-shell stock,
with alkaline bite prized. **Malaysia went dry**, with caramelised dark soy darkening the
noodles, smaller pork-based wontons, char siu as standard, and the soup demoted to a side bowl.
That dry dark-soy form **has no Guangzhou antecedent** and should be treated as a Malayan
innovation. Nobody has dated when or why it happened. **Confidence: high** on the description,
**medium** on the divergence.

**In Penang specifically:** a Cantonese-minority dish on a Hokkien-majority island, which is why
it reads as slightly imported next to Hokkien mee or CKT. Penang's is somewhat less dark than
Klang Valley versions, with the pickled chilli prominent, and some stalls do a chilli-sauce-forward
"red" version. The kon lo half of the dish is still open on the checklist and it is a genuinely
different eating experience, not a variation.

## 9 · Curry Mee (coconut) — Restoran 77 Food Yard · 28 Jul 2026

*咖喱麵 ka-lí mī. Malay mi kari. South of Penang and in Singapore, curry laksa or laksa lemak.*

**Noodle:** yellow alkaline wheat and/or bee hoon, mixed on request.

**Follow the word.** Tamil *kari* கறி "sauce" → Portuguese *caril* → English *curry* →
phonetically back into Chinese as 咖喱 → into a Hokkien mouth. Four languages for one word before
it reaches the stall.

**The bowl:** broth of prawn shells, coconut milk and curry spices. **Coagulated pig's blood
cubes** — the Penang signature — cuttlefish, prawns, cockles, tofu puffs, long beans, bean
sprouts, mint, and a **separately served sambal** the eater stirs in to taste.

**A four-way fusion you can decompose.**

| Component | Thread |
| --- | --- |
| Yellow noodle, bee hoon | Chinese (Hokkien) |
| Curry spice base | South Indian / Indian Muslim, **enabled by British-commodified curry powder** |
| Coconut milk, sambal, belacan, daun kesum | Malay and Nyonya |
| Pig's blood, pork, lard | Chinese, and specifically **non-halal** |

That last row is the sting: a dish with Indian spice and Malay coconut is fixed permanently on
the Chinese side of the halal boundary by cubes of blood. Fusion in Penang decorates boundaries;
it does not dissolve them.

**Named after its noodle.** It is curry *mee* where yellow noodle or bee hoon is used and curry
*laksa* where thick round rice noodle is used. Same dish, and the noodle picks the name. The
cleanest example in the dataset of that naming logic.

**Penang against elsewhere.** Against Singapore laksa lemak: the pork blood and the stir-it-in
sambal are Penang's markers; Singapore's is thicker with coconut, on thick laksa noodles, with
fish cake, cockles and tau pok. Against Klang Valley curry laksa: deep-fried tofu, cockles, long
beans and mint as signatures, and generally richer and more coconut-forward. Penang broths,
especially at older stalls, are notably **thinner and less coconut-heavy** — which is the
substrate for the white curry mee phenomenon.

**Contested, and thinly.** The oldest documented Penang lineage is the Air Itam stall of the Lim
sisters, whose mother was selling by **1946** — but that dates a *stall*, not a dish. The
**Hainanese** claim is repeated widely in Malaysian food writing and demonstrated nowhere for
Penang curry mee specifically; the mechanism is documented only for Singapore's Katong laksa. The
santan-rempah-daun-kesum architecture is unambiguously **Peranakan**, but no specific household
invention claim survives scrutiny. And the line that curry mee "became popular in the 1970s–80s
among workers needing affordable filling meals" is content-farm material, contradicted by the
1946 evidence. **Confidence: medium** overall.

## 10 · Koay Teow Th'ng (Instant Cook) — Sin Hup Aun Cafe · 29 Jul 2026
## 15 · Koay Teow Th'ng (Ah Liang) — Pulau Tikus Hidden Food Court · 11 Aug 2026

*粿條湯 kóe-tiâu thng. Malay sup kuetiau.*

Taken together, because two stalls in the same series is the point.

**Noodle:** flat rice noodle, usually a thinner cut than CKT's. Some stalls offer bee hoon or
mixed.

**The bowl:** a deliberately **clear** broth — the aesthetic opposite of char kway teow —
simmered for hours from pork, chicken and/or duck bones, very often with **dried flounder** (*ti
poh* 地魚) and sometimes soybeans or dried anchovy for depth. Fish balls and fish cake from fish
forcemeat, minced pork, pork slices, offal, lettuce, spring onion, fried garlic and garlic oil,
white pepper. Chilli in light soy on the side.

**The diaspora, in two syllables.** Koay teow th'ng, Vietnam's ***hủ tiếu*** — and note *hủ tiếu
Nam Vang*, literally "Phnom Penh koay teow" — Cambodia's ***kuy teav***, and Thai
***ก๋วยเตี๋ยว kuaitiao*** are all the same two Teochew syllables. Thai *บะหมี่ bamee* is a loan
of Teochew *bah-mī*. **The Thai words for rice noodle and egg noodle are Chinese dialect words**,
which settles the direction of transmission and makes the Teochew diaspora legible as a
distribution network rather than a scatter of isolated communities. **Confidence: high**, and it
is one of the strongest single pieces of evidence in the whole dataset.

**Penang's three sub-types:** pork, duck, and a less common chicken. Duck is the prestige option,
historically associated with Lorong Ngah Aboo off Kimberley Street — *Swatow Kay*, the Teochew
street. Penang bowls are meatier than Singapore's, which leans on fish balls as the point of the
dish. Ipoh's *kai si hor fun* is a genuinely different dish that outsiders conflate with it:
different dialect group, different stock philosophy, different protein.

**No inventor claim exists**, which is itself informative — this is a *base*, not a *creation*.
The clear-broth-plus-blanched-noodle template is the substrate that lor mee thickens, that lam
mee enriches with prawn stock, and that wantan mee serves on the side.

**Why two episodes is right.** Penang Institute's work on hawker transmission gives the
vocabulary: informal apprenticeship, recipe drift, and ***air tangan*** — the notion that the
same recipe in different hands produces a different bowl. Two stalls, nominally one dish, two
distinguishable bowls. That is not inconsistency in the dish; it is the mechanism by which the
dish exists at all, since none of this was ever written down.

## 11 · Lor Mee — Cheah Yew Market · 30 Jul 2026

*滷麵 ló͘-mī. Philippines lomi. Not to be confused with Cantonese lo mein 撈麵, a different word.*

**Noodle:** yellow alkaline wheat and/or bee hoon.

**Etymology, and the concept.** 滷 *ló͘* is the master-stock braise word — the same 滷 as in *lor
bak* and *lor tan*, a dark spiced soy braising liquid with five-spice, star anise, cassia and
garlic. In lor mee the braising liquid is thickened with starch. **lor is a technique, not a
dish**, which is worth holding onto because it explains why the *lor* family sprawls across
Teochew braised duck, koay chiap and Hokkien noodles alike.

**The bowl in Penang:** a five-spice-scented, pourable starch-thickened gravy over noodle, with
prawn, pork, egg and fish cake.

**Not the same dish as Singapore lor mee.** Singapore's is near-gelatinous, with *ngo hiang*
five-spice pork rolls, fried fish bits, meat dumplings, half a braised egg, and — critically —
**black vinegar and raw minced garlic added at the table**. Penang's is soupier and lighter, and
described locally as a distinct Penang dish formed under Hokkien and (per Malay Mail, which is
journalism rather than scholarship) Hainanese influence.

**The important local fact.** Penang lor mee is usually sold at the **same stall as Penang
Hokkien mee**, off the same prawn-and-pork stock thickened with tapioca flour and egg. That
efficiency has quietly hybridised the two dishes on the island — which is why Penang lor mee is
arguably a derivative of Penang Hokkien mee as much as a direct Zhangzhou transplant. The dataset
carries this as a `co_sold_with` edge, and it is a good example of why that edge type earns its
place.

**Contested.** The **Zhangzhou** attribution is repeated across sources and no primary
Chinese-language documentation surfaced. Putian's own lor mee is lighter, less starched and
seafood-based, which suggests the thick-gravy form is regionally specific *within* Fujian rather
than universal. Watch also for the false friend: Henan 卤面 uses the same characters for a
completely unrelated dish. **Confidence: medium.**

**And a caution.** Do not read lor mee's warm sweet spices as Arab-derived. **Five-spice is
Chinese and star anise is a Chinese native.** Cassia, clove, cardamom and star anise circulated on
the same Indian Ocean routes for a millennium, so the register has genuinely convergent Chinese
and Arab-Indian sources. Convergence, not influence.

## 12 · Mee Rebus — 33 Best Food Hub, Tanjung Tokong · 31 Jul 2026

*Mi rebus. Chinese-Malaysian 马来卤面, "Malay lor mee" — a small masterpiece of cross-community
classification.*

**Noodle:** yellow alkaline wheat — Chinese in origin, which is why some sources argue for
Chinese influence on the dish.

**Etymology.** Malay *rebus*, "to boil or blanch." **Boiled noodles** — named, like lam mee, for a
process rather than a flavour.

**The bowl:** blanched noodles under *kuah* — a thick gravy of *grago* shrimp, flour, sugar and
salt, aromatised with lemongrass, ginger and shallots, enriched with mutton, prawns, ikan bilis
or even flower crab, **thickened with mashed sweet potato**, sometimes with ground peanut, and
seasoned with **curry powder, taucu and palm sugar**. Bean sprouts, halved hard-boiled egg, tau
kwa, fried shallots, sliced green chilli, a drizzle of dark soy, calamansi on the side.

**Contested — four live theories.** Derived from *mie jawa*, though NLB notes the Javanese dish
"bears little resemblance to mee rebus in its current form." Carried south from the northern
states by **Indian Muslim peddlers** cooking to order from a mobile stove and containers slung on
a **kandar pole** — the same apparatus that gave Penang nasi kandar; NLB's own opening line
commits to this, which makes it the strongest single attribution. Created in **Singapore before
WWII**, per food historian Khir Johari. Or a fifteenth-to-seventeenth-century Penang Peranakan
dish, which is implausible on its face since alkaline yellow wheat noodles were not a mass
commodity here then. **Confidence: medium**, with the Indian-Muslim account leading.

**Regional variants:** mee rebus Johor uses freshly ground tapioca and pure beef stock, and
purists insist tapioca flour or prawn stock makes it inauthentic. Klang Valley uses a *dhal*-based
gravy. **Mee bandung**, from Muar, is a close relative — beef stock, prawns, chilli, sweet-potato
thickening. Kuala Terengganu has its own.

**The mee rebus / mee jawa comparison** is set out in full in Chapter 5. Short version: they
share the noodle, the sweet-potato thickening, the ground peanut, the garnish set and the lime;
they differ on the halal line, on taucu versus tomato, on whether the gravy is actively spiced,
and on whether fritters are integral. **Model them as siblings with mutual influence, not parent
and child.** NLB treats mee jawa as a variant of mee rebus; Penang treats them as separate
dishes; both are defensible, which usually means the question is badly posed.

**One transmission mechanism in plain sight:** NLB records that mee rebus stalls "usually also
offer mee goreng" because the ingredients overlap. Co-selling is how recipes actually move.

## 13 · Lemak Laksa (Granny Q) — Sin Yong Wah, Pulau Tikus · 7 Aug 2026

*Laksa lemak, laksa Nyonya. In Penang sometimes called "Siamese laksa."*

**Noodle:** thick round rice laksa noodle.

**Etymology.** *lemak* is a Malay culinary descriptor rather than a dish name: it denotes the
specific quality of richness that coconut milk imparts. *Nyonya* signals Peranakan provenance and
the fact that Peranakan cuisine is women's cuisine.

**The bowl:** coconut-milk gravy over a rempah of shallot, garlic, candlenut, lemongrass,
galangal, turmeric, dried chilli and belacan; dried shrimp; prawn, fish cake, tau pok, bean
sprouts, egg; daun kesum; sambal on the side.

**Its position in Penang, which is the interesting part.** Decisively secondary to asam laksa,
and sometimes sold as a half-and-half hybrid in the same bowl. But note what Penang cooks *call*
it: "Siamese laksa." Since Thai laksa is the coconut one, that label is internal evidence that
Penang's own self-understanding places the **sour, coconut-free style as the local norm and the
creamy style as the imported other**. A community's taxonomy tells you what it thinks it is.

**Relationship to curry mee — the crux.** Laksa lemak and curry mee/curry laksa are the **same
coconut-curry-noodle family**, distinguished by regional convention, noodle choice and toppings
rather than by any hard line. Wikipedia says outright that "laksa" is an alternate name for curry
mee. Any claim of directional descent between them is unsupported. **Confidence: medium** — the family
resemblance is uncontroversial, everything downstream of it is not.

| | Penang curry mee | KL curry laksa | Singapore laksa lemak / Katong |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Noodle | yellow mee + bee hoon | yellow mee + bee hoon | thick laksa noodle (cut short in Katong) |
| Coconut | moderate to light | heavy | heavy |
| Sambal | separate, stir in | often stirred in | separate |
| Signature | **pork blood**, cuttlefish | fried tofu, cockles, long beans, mint | ground dried shrimp thickening |
| Herb | mint | mint | daun kesum |

**And the transmission mechanism worth carrying away.** Katong laksa's street career is
documented: **Hainanese domestic workers** who cooked for Peranakan employers learned the
household laksa and, when jobs were scarce after 1945, took it onto the street. As Tony Boey
observes, no Peranakan family would have permitted an heirloom recipe to be peddled as street
food. The cook was the leak — and the same channel explains Hainanese chicken rice, chicken chop
and mee Hailam.

## 14 · Dry Sabah Pan Mee, fried pork — Sabah Pan Mee, Pulau Tikus · 10 Aug 2026

Same dough, same stall, different dish — and the dry fried-pork configuration is worth
distinguishing, because it points somewhere the soup version does not.

Sabah's actual signature pork noodle is ***sang nyuk mee*** 生肉麵, "fresh meat noodle": yellow
egg noodle or bee hoon served either in a peppery clear pork broth or ***kon lau*** 乾撈,
dry-tossed in dark soy and rendered pork lard with lard croutons and the soup on the side.
Reported as invented in **1979 by two Chinese brothers in Tawau**, on Sabah's east coast.
**Confidence: medium** — single-lineage claim, no archival corroboration.

Which means the dry version eaten here sits closer to Sabah's real noodle culture than the soup
version does, even though neither is a dish called "Sabah pan mee." And it puts the bowl inside
a much larger architecture: **minced or sliced pork over dry-tossed noodle with soup on the
side** is shared by Sabah's sang nyuk mee kon lau, Sarawak's kolo mee, peninsular Hakka mee, KL's
Soong Kee dry Hakka beef noodle, and Teochew dry bak chor mee. Three dialect groups, one
architecture, arrived at independently or by mutual borrowing — which is why the dataset carries
`shares_architecture` as an edge type distinct from descent.

## 16 · Mee Sotong Sambal (Jones Road) — Sin Hup Aun Cafe, Pulau Tikus · 12 Aug 2026

*Mee sotong / mee goreng sotong. This stall trades as Jones Road Famous Mee Sotong Sambal.*

**Noodle:** yellow alkaline wheat, blanched then wok-tossed.

**The bowl:** a reddish sambal gravy clinging to the noodles — sweet, tangy and hot at once —
with cuttlefish cooked *into* the sauce rather than laid on top, plus bean sprouts, egg, potato,
fried tofu, fried shallots, and a calamansi wedge that is not optional.

**Why this one matters to the series.** It is the **first Tamil-Muslim-carried bowl in sixteen
episodes**. Before it the run was nine Hokkien bowls, three Hakka, three Teochew, three
Peranakan, one Cantonese, one Hainanese, one Javanese — and nothing at all from the community
that invented the mamak stall. The largest single gap in the coverage map closed with one lunch.

**Threads:** the noodle is Chinese, bought from the same factories that supply every other stall
on the island. The wok and the stir-fry format are Chinese. The sambal, the tamarind-sweet
balance and the calamansi are Malay. The dish is halal, urban and Indian-Muslim. This is
mechanism two from Chapter 6 — *halal boundary work* — in its purest form: keep the Chinese
noodle, taugeh, tofu, egg and wok; remove the pork and lard; rebuild the seasoning from the
South Indian and Malay side. What comes out is not a compromise, it is a new dish.

**Two lineages, and it is worth keeping them apart.** **Hameed "Pata"** at Kota Selera, Padang
Kota Lama, whose family account has the father selling mee rebus and mee goreng from 1942 with
the sotong specialisation dating to 1978, leads with a dark squid sauce. **Jones Road** — from
the Jalan Jones / Tingkat Jones T-junction in Pulau Tikus since the 1980s, now operating out of
Sin Hup Aun Cafe — leads with sambal gravy. That wet/dry split runs right through Penang mee
goreng generally, and it is the reason the island's mamak fried noodles read so differently from
the Klang Valley's.

**A note on the stall, which is the more interesting fact.** Jones Road is a *relocated cook*,
not a relocated shop. The name travelled with him into somebody else's kopitiam, and the kopitiam
in question is the same Sin Hup Aun Cafe that produced the Instant Cook koay teow th'ng in
episode 10. That is how most Penang hawker lineages actually survive — not as premises but as a
person with a wok and a reputation, renting a corner.

**Confidence: medium.** Both lineages are stall-sourced family history with no archival support,
which is the norm for this genre and the rule for mamak dishes in particular. Read 1942, 1978 and
"the 1980s" as tradition rather than record.

# Part two: still on the list

Shorter entries, with the one thing worth knowing before ordering.

## Island classics

**White Curry Mee** — Both a real tradition and a marketing category, and the marketing came
second. Sambal-on-the-side is the *normal* Penang curry mee convention, attested to the 1940s
stall generation, and Tony Boey names the Air Itam sisters among the pioneers while stating
plainly that nobody knows who invented it. But "Penang white curry noodles" became an
international *category* through **MyKuali**, founded by Penangite Thomas Tang, whose instant
product launched in 2012 and was ranked first in The Ramen Rater's Top 10 of All Time in 2014.
Model it as a real serving convention that was named, reified and globalised by the
instant-noodle industry. Flag any source presenting it as an ancient named tradition.

**Asam Laksa** — The flagship, and the dish with the most unresolved question attached. No
coconut milk at all. *Ikan kembung* flaked into strands, tamarind and asam gelugur, a rempah of
dried chilli and lemongrass and belacan, and the garnish battery: pineapple, cucumber, raw onion,
mint, shredded **bunga kantan**, and a dark swirl of **hae ko**. Ranked #7 on CNN Go's World's 50
Most Delicious Foods on **21 July 2011** — the only Malaysian entry in the top ten. (Re-promoted
in 2020, which is why half the internet misdates it.) The open question is direction of travel
against **laksa Kedah**: Penang was Kedah until 1786 and the island's Malay substrate is Kedahan,
so a Peranakan elaboration of an existing Kedah sour fish laksa is at least as plausible as the
reverse. The English-language habit of narrating Penang as origin is probably prominence bias.

**Mee Goreng Mamak** — The four-syllable fusion: Chinese noun, Malay verb, Tamil honorific. Not
found in India. Penang credited as origin, early 1900s, with **Bangkok Lane** in Pulau Tikus among
the oldest, from a pushcart, now ninety-plus years old — and the Penang style is notably *wet*,
with gravy borrowed from the pasembur stall next door. The community distinction matters: Tamil
**Muslim** migration was mercantile and urban, and being Muslim they could sell to Malays and buy
Chinese noodles. Halal is the enabling constraint, not an incidental attribute.

**Maggi Goreng** — Mee goreng mamak with the noodle swapped. The only dish in this dataset with a
supply-chain birthday: Maggi reached Malaysia in **1969** with tomato ketchup and chilli sauce and
in **1971** with two-minute noodles in Kari and Ayam. It cannot predate 1971. Note the sequence —
the sauce that defines the dish arrived two years before the noodle that names it. No inventor is
recorded and none should be accepted.

**Bee Hoon** — 米粉, and the international name for the product is a **Hokkien word**: both "bee
hoon" and Malay "bihun" descend from 米粉 read in Southern Min, which is a direct index of who
carried it around the world. In Penang, char bee hoon is the backbone of the **economy bee hoon**
breakfast tray. Distinguish it from *tang hoon*, the mung-bean glass noodle, which English menus
routinely blur. And if the board says "Singapore noodles," that dish is from Hong Kong.

**Koay Chiak → actually koay chiap** — 粿汁. 粿 rice sheet + 汁 gravy — and 汁 is *not* 湯 soup,
which distinguishes it from koay teow th'ng at the level of the name. Broad flat rice sheets,
folded loose, in a dark *lou* braise. **Penang substitutes duck for pork** — duck meat and offal
alongside pig's ear, braised egg and beancurd — consistent with the island's Teochew hawkers
having built their whole repertoire around duck. Evening and supper trade. The best datum:
the **Chaoshan home form has a white, opaque broth made with rice milk**; the Straits form is a
dark soy braise; the Thai form is clear. One diaspora dish, three countries, three broths — and
the ancestral one is not the dark one.

**Bak Chor Mee** — Hard to find here, and the reasons are structural. Penang's Teochew community
is real but far smaller than Singapore's; Penang's Teochew hawkers took the **rice-noodle** niche
(koay teow th'ng, koay chiap) rather than the wheat one; and the defining chilli-and-black-vinegar
dressing is a **1950s Singapore development**, postdating the era when Penang and Singapore
hawker repertoires were most freely exchanged. The lineage is the best-provenanced in the corpus:
**Chen Lianfu** left **Zhao'an county, Fujian** — not Chaozhou — in the late 1910s, learned the
trade in Chaozhou city, and hawked in Chai Chee from the 1920s from a shoulder-pole stall
following street-opera crowds; the load bent his back and the stall became 驼背面, "Hunchback
Noodle." Eleven descendant stalls still operate and one supplier still makes the noodle.

**Duck noodle / duck koay teow** — Teochew, and duck is the Teochew meat of choice. Penang keeps
the **soup clear and the meat braised dark** — two techniques in one bowl, deliberately not
mixed — where Singapore more often uses the braising liquid itself as the broth. Best-documented
Penang lineage: **Lum Lai**, founded late 1970s by Lau Lum Lai as an itinerant pushcart, now at
Cecil Street Market with a Michelin Bib. Bangkok's *bamee ped tun* uses the same five-spice braise
from the same migration, so the two are **siblings, not ancestor and descendant**.

**Beef noodles** — Four unrelated traditions share the English name, and keeping them apart is the
whole exercise. Lanzhou (1915, Ma Baozi, Hui Muslim, five-element municipal standard — the
best-documented origin anywhere in this dataset). Taiwanese red-braised (created *in Taiwan* by
KMT veterans from Sichuan in the Gangshan military villages after 1949). Malaysian clear or
herbal (a **1930s–40s Malayan hawker development** made by Hainanese *and* Hakka hawkers — Soong
Kee in KL, 1945, traces its dish to **Dabu Hakka noodles**, not Hainanese cooking at all). And
Malay *soto daging* and *sup tulang*, which is where the halal beef-noodle position actually
sits. **Disputed.** The line that Hainanese beef noodles came from Hainan is not evidenced;
Hainan's own cuisine is not notably beef-centric. Penang's beef-noodle scene is thin, squeezed
from both sides — Chinese beef stalls are not halal, and many Chinese Malaysians of Cantonese and
Hakka background avoid beef entirely on Buddhist and Taoist grounds.

**Fish head bee hoon / fish soup noodles** — A **Singapore** dish, weakly represented here.
Penang's Teochew hawkers occupy the adjacent niche with fish-ball koay teow th'ng, and Penang's
fish-noodle energy goes into asam laksa — same ingredients, completely different idiom, and a
useful reminder that ingredient overlap is not kinship. On the white version: genuine emulsion
comes from long-boiled bones; the common route is added **evaporated milk**, which Teochew purists
reject. The blog claim that the milk was a Hainanese British-influenced innovation is unsourced.

**Chee cheong fun** — The clearest single example in the Penang repertoire of a **Cantonese
carrier dish reseasoned with a Malay-archipelago fermented-seafood condiment**. Same steamed rice
sheet as Hong Kong, KL and Ipoh; radically different flavour vector. Penang is the *only* version
whose sauce is built on fermented shrimp — **hae ko** blended with *thnee cheo*, plus sesame,
fried shallot and chilli — rather than on soy, bean or sesame. KL uses sweet fermented bean
paste; Ipoh runs a dry version with dried shrimp and a wet one flooded with curry; Hong Kong
treats the sheet as the point and the sauce as incidental. And 豬腸粉, "pig intestine noodle,"
contains no offal — the name describes the shape.

## Regional and immigrant bowls

**Ban mian** — See episode 2. If a stall brands it separately, the honest distinction is between
the three *forming methods* (torn, cut flat, cut round) rather than between two dishes.

**Mee Soto** — The clearest Javanese node on the island, and the one that arrived as a **whole
dish** rather than as ingredients. Turmeric-lemongrass-galangal chicken broth, shredded chicken,
bean sprouts, and near-obligatory **begedil**, which is itself a Dutch *frikadel* loan into
Indonesian. And *soto* may derive from a Chinese offal-soup term acculturated in nineteenth-century
Java, which means soto was **already a Sino-Javanese creole dish** before it became a Malay dish in
Penang. Three empires and two diasporas in one bowl, none of them on the signboard. Its cousin in
Suriname is *saoto*.

**You mee** — Not a dish. Ask the stall which of four things they mean: 幼麵 *yau min*, the thin
round egg noodle identical to mee kia and wonton noodle (this is what a pan mee shop means, and it
is a noodle *option*); 伊麵 *yee mee*, the fried dried e-fu brick; 魚麵, a real Foochow fish-paste
noodle from Sibu and Sitiawan; or 油麵, an alias for ordinary yellow noodle. Nearly every English
food blog using the phrase does so without specifying.

**Cantonese yi mein / e-fu** — 伊麵, "the Yi family's noodle," after **Yi Bingshou** (1754–1815),
Qing calligrapher, poet and prefect of Huizhou. Boiled or steamed, then deep-fried and dried into
a brick, then rehydrated — a shelf-stable pre-cooked noodle. The canonical banquet form is 乾燒伊麵,
braised dry with shiitake and **yellow Chinese chives**, both near-obligatory. A longevity noodle:
served at birthdays and New Year, strands not to be cut, and specifically the main dish at Hakka
birthday banquets. In Malaysia "yee mee" is everyday coffeeshop food. Note: calling it the ancestor
of instant noodles is rhetoric, not descent.

**Ipoh hor fun** — Cantonese in origin, and the good complication is that Ipoh Echo reports the two
most celebrated *kai si hor fun* stalls, **Thean Chun** and the Loke Wooi Kee stall, were founded
by **Hokkien** immigrants from **Nan'an, Fujian** — which is why the broth combines chicken *and*
prawn, in the Hokkien manner, with **Tanjung Tualang prawn shells** giving the orange tint. That
prawn element has no Chinese antecedent; it is local. And the universally repeated **limestone
water** claim is not evidenced for noodles: the geology and the hard water are real, the
bean-sprout version is the strongest of the family and even that is asserted rather than
demonstrated, and rice-noodle texture is dominated by rice variety, flour age, hydration and
steaming. Honest formulation: Ipoh has a concentrated, competitive, multi-generational noodle trade
and attributes its results to the water.

**Thai boat noodles / tom yum noodles** — Both recent arrivals, and important not to back-date.
Boat noodles: both pork and beef in one bowl, dark soy, fermented bean curd, cinnamon and star
anise, and the defining thickener, **fresh pig's or cow's blood**. The small bowl has a good
functional explanation — one vendor paddled, cooked, served, took money and washed up alone, and a
large bowl would spill in the hand-off from boat to bank. But claims of twelfth-to-sixteenth-century
Ayutthaya origins are anachronistic: the dish's name is a Teochew loanword, mass Teochew settlement
in Siam dates from the late eighteenth century, and Rangsit's canals were dug in the **1890s**. Tom
yum noodles, by contrast, are thoroughly naturalised in Penang: **Raja Uda and Butterworth** are a
recognised cluster, the Penang style is a thick red chilli-paste-heavy broth with lime and bunga
kantan against the clear herb-forward Thai style, and customers pick from a tray, which is a
Malaysian convention. Ghee Lian on Perak Road opened in 2012 with a **green** tom yum noodle:
local invention inside an imported category.

**Sarawak laksa** — Thin **bee hoon**, not thick laksa noodle. A composite rempah — garlic,
shallot, lemongrass, galangal, **peanuts**, candlenuts, chilli, coriander, cumin, fennel, sesame,
cinnamon, star anise, pepper, belacan — over prawn stock, chicken stock, coconut milk and
tamarind, finished with sambal belacan and calamansi. Tony Boey's component reading: prawn stock
from the Hokkien prawn noodle, chicken stock Cantonese, coconut Nyonya, peanut from Malay satay
sauce. **Kuching, not Sibu**: the credited originator, Goh Lik Teck, was **Teochew**, peddling on
Carpenter Street in the 1940s — single-sourced to a 2015 state tourism guide chapter that never
describes the dish, so continuity with today's version is unverifiable. And the real mechanism of
standardisation is industrial: **Tan Yong Him's Swallow-brand rempah premix** from the 1960s
created and froze the modern dish, thirty years before MyKuali did the same for white curry mee.
Bourdain called it the Breakfast of the Gods at Choon Hui Café in 2005, two mornings running, and
that is why anyone outside Borneo has heard of it.

**Bihun goreng / char beehoon** — Three parallel traditions, one noodle, no merging. Chinese *char
bee hoon* is soy-forward and comparatively pale. Malay *bihun goreng* is built on a pounded
dried-chilli rempah with kicap manis and tomato. Mamak *bihun goreng* adds bottled tomato ketchup
and chilli sauce — sweeter and tangier than the Malay style, milder than mee goreng mamak, finished
with lime. They coexist street by street because of the halal boundary. That is the whole point:
**the noodle is shared infrastructure, the seasoning is where the community lives.**

## Wild cards

**Chilli pan mee** — A documented **1985** KL invention by **Tan Kok Hong** at **Kin Kin**, from a
stall under a tree in Chow Kit: he sold conventional pan mee in clear pork broth, noticed customers
spooning chilli in, and built a dry version around a chilli condiment he still does not sell
separately; the runny poached egg came later. The evidence base is his own testimony repeated
across food media for three decades with no competing claimant — and Wikipedia's citation for
"invented in Chow Kit" is a 2017 food blog, which is the citation-laundering pattern to watch for.
**It is not a traditional Hakka dish**, whatever the blogs say. It is a 1980s invention on a Hakka
substrate.

**Seasonal / festival noodles** — Mee sua in sesame-oil-and-rice-wine or clear chicken broth with a
**red-dyed hard-boiled egg**. Long unbroken strands, never cut, ideally each strand eaten whole.
The red egg extends the full-month newborn custom to all birthdays. Chives go in because Hokkiens
hear 韮 *gau* as 久 *gau*, "a long time." The Foochow variant is *ang jiu mee sua*, in red
glutinous-rice-wine lees chicken soup, standard for birthdays, confinement and New Year in Sitiawan
and Sarawak. And in Penang Nyonya households the birthday noodle is **lam mee** instead — the same
idea, a different noodle, a different dialect's answer.

## Not on the checklist, but on any complete Penang noodle map

**Penang Hokkien Char** 福建炒 — yellow mee plus bee hoon, only a tinge of dark soy, prawns, pork,
egg, bean sprout, chives, smoky *wok hei* over sauce. Penangites use the name precisely to avoid
the collision with Penang Hokkien mee, which is a soup. Closer in spirit to char kway teow than to
its KL namesake.

**Mee Sotong** — tasted, episode 16; see Part one. Left in this section as a marker of how the
map changes: it was filed here as "belongs on any complete Penang noodle map but is not on the
checklist" right up until it became the bowl that closed the series' biggest gap.

**Mee Udang** — Whole unpeeled prawns in tomato-and-chilli gravy with tamarind. **Teluk Kumbar** on
the island's southwest coast is the historic cluster and has become a style name used island-wide;
**Sungai Dua** on the mainland, near the Muda estuary fishery, is the second. Timothy Tye's
description is precise and useful: "primarily a Malay dish similar to the Jawa Mee sold by Chinese
hawkers." **No source documents when or by whom it was established at Sungai Dua** — the clearest
single instance of the evidence asymmetry between Chinese and Malay hawker history.

**Mee Siam** — There is no such dish in Thai cuisine. Khir Johari's reading is the most economical:
the name refers to the **Thai-imported dried vermicelli**, not the dish, exactly as *laksa*
originally named a noodle. Four named disputants on origin — Hutton (Penang), Sylvia Tan (Malay),
Tan Chee-Beng (wholly Peranakan), Chua Beng Huat (the question is malformed) — and the dataset
carries all four rather than resolving them. First Singapore newspaper mention: **1950**, hawkers at
the Esplanade selling to evening strollers. Note the *taucu + tamarind + dried shrimp* triad — it is
exactly mee rebus's flavour spine, minus the sweet potato.

**Char Koay Kak** 炒粿角 — Cubes of steamed rice-flour cake wok-fried with egg, bean sprouts, chives,
preserved radish and dark soy. Attributed to Penang's Teochew community, who brought the steamed
rice-cake tradition from southern China, with traces of Hokkien influence in the ingredients. The
same 粿 technology as char kway teow, cut into cubes instead of ribbons.

**Mee Hailam** 海南麵 — Stir-fried yellow noodles with meat or seafood and abundant vegetables in a
dark-soy gravy, with calamansi. A **Malayan invention by Hainanese cooks**, not a transplant from
Hainan — the noodle equivalent of chicken chop and "Western food." What happens when the only
dialect group whose economic niche literally *was* cooking, and cooking for non-Chinese employers,
opens its own shop.

**Wat Tan Hor** 滑蛋河 — Noodles fried hard and dry first to build *wok hei* and char the edges —
essential — then covered with cornflour-thickened stock into which beaten egg is drizzled in ribbons
at the last second. **No named inventor and no origin legend at all**, which is itself notable: wat
tan hor is a technique dish, not a story dish. The *moonlight* version sets a raw yolk on top for the
diner to stir through.

**Pasembur / Mee Rojak** — Shredded cucumber and *sengkuang*, bean sprouts, potato, boiled egg, fried
tofu, prawn and flour fritters, drenched in an orange-red gravy of mashed sweet potato, ground
peanuts, chilli and tamarind — with a yellow-noodle version sold as mee rojak. Origin genuinely
three-way disputed. And this is where **mee jawa and mee rojak meet**: the same flavour space,
reached from a Chinese kitchen on one side and an Indian-Muslim one on the other, on the same
street, for a century. No single source states the relationship. The dataset carries it as
`shares_architecture` plus a mutual `influenced_by`, and the gap in the literature is itself the
finding.
