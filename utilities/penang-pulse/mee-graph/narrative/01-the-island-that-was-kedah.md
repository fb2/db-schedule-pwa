# 1. The island that was Kedah

There is a habit in food writing of treating Penang as a Chinese island with interesting
neighbours. Almost everything that is confusing about Penang noodles follows from that one
mistake.

Start again from the deed. On 9 July 1786 Francis Light concluded a provisional agreement
with Sultan Abdullah Mukarram Shah of Kedah, and on 11 August he landed on an island that
was, until that morning, Kedah territory. What Light offered was military protection
against Siamese and Burmese pressure — which tells you immediately that the political
horizon here ran north to Bangkok and Ava, not south to Malacca and Johor. He did not
deliver the protection. The Sultan assembled a force at Prai in 1790 to take the island
back and was defeated; a treaty in 1791 confirmed the cession against an annual payment of
six thousand Spanish dollars. In 1800 Lieutenant-Governor George Leith acquired the
mainland strip that became Province Wellesley, and with it the colony's food supply: rice,
sugar, livestock, vegetables.

Three consequences run all the way down into the bowls.

**The Malay substrate here is Kedah Malay.** Not Johor-Riau Malay. Kedah is Malaysia's rice
bowl, and its foodways are the northern ones: freshwater and inshore fish, tamarind sour,
dried *asam gelugur* slices, herbal top notes. Penang's Malay dishes are best read as
urbanised Kedah dishes, and Penang's Nyonya dishes are best read as Chinese-Malay creole
cooking built on a *Kedah* Malay base. When people say Penang Nyonya food is "Thai
influenced," what they are usually noticing is that Kedah was a Siamese tributary for
centuries and that the sour-and-fiery register of the far north of the peninsula does not
stop politely at a border drawn in the twentieth century. Kuroda's work on the Samsam —
Tai-speaking, mostly Muslim populations of inland Kedah, recorded by nineteenth-century
English observers in both Kedah and Penang — describes a boundary that was gradual,
linguistic and religious rather than a line. The honest framing is not *borrowing across a
border* but *one northern-peninsula regional cuisine*, of which Penang is the port city.

**The mainland and the island diverge from the start.** Province Wellesley took the estate
labour — Tamil, Javanese, Sumatran — in a way George Town did not. That is why the
"mainland" dishes of the Penang food map have a Malay and Javanese centre of gravity —
lontong, mee jawa, mee rebus, mee udang out at Sungai Dua near the Muda estuary — while the
island's canon is Chinese and Peranakan. Cross the bridge and the food changes because the
labour history changes. Anyone working through a Penang noodle checklist will feel this
without being told: the mainland picks read as a different cuisine, and they are.

**And Penang was never a Malaysian city.** It was the commercial capital of a maritime
region whose other corners were southern Siam and North Sumatra. By the mid-nineteenth
century Penang's Big Five Hokkien families ran a supply circuit pushing rice, brown sugar,
coconut oil, cloth and salted vegetables into the tin mines of Perak and Phuket and the
tobacco plantations of Deli. Hokkien migration to the Siamese tin frontier came
substantially *via* Penang, producing the Phuket Baba community — locally called
Baba-Nyonyá — and something similar at Ranong. On the Sumatran side, Medan Hokkien is a
close relative of Penang Hokkien. Ingredients, cooks, brides and recipes moved along a
north–south Indian Ocean axis, not a national one.

This is the single most useful thing to hold in mind while eating here. Penang food
resembles southern Thai and North Sumatran food more than it resembles Johor food, and it
does so for structural reasons rather than atmospheric ones. In the dataset this is the concept node
`x-northern-triangle`, and the cultures and dishes that hang off it — Hokkien commerce, the
Penang Peranakan kitchen, asam laksa's sour register — are the ones hardest to explain any
other way.

## What the island was made of

By 1786 there was already a Chinese presence in the region, and it was commercially
organised. Light did not import settlers from China. He recruited them from next door.
**Koh Lay Huan** — a Hokkien merchant who had rebelled against the Qing, fled through Siam,
and based himself at Kuala Muda in Kedah — brought "several boatloads of Chinese and Malay
settlers from Kedah" to clear land and establish trade, and Light appointed him the first
and only Kapitan Cina of George Town in May 1787. Koh's eldest son later served as Siamese
governor over Kuala Kedah. Penang's founding Chinese elite were not fresh migrants; they
were regional political operators who happened to relocate.

At the same time, Peranakan Baba families began moving north from Dutch Malacca, attracted
by the new free port. So the Chinese community here was layered from day one: a
Malacca-Peranakan and Kedah-Hokkien mercantile stratum on top, and direct arrivals from
Fujian and Guangdong underneath, arriving in volume later. That stratification is the
origin of a distinction you can still taste — between *Nyonya* home cooking, with its
pounded spice pastes and tamarind, and *sinkeh* hawker cooking, with its stock pots and soy.
They are not two points on one spectrum. They are two different culinary logics that
arrived in Penang at different times and married into each other.

Tamil Muslim traders were also already working the Kedah coast, and arrived in Penang within
days of Light's landing. Hadhrami Arabs had been trading in these ports since roughly the
thirteenth century. The Siamese and Burmese Buddhist communities of Pulau Tikus date to the
first decades: the Burmese temple's land is recorded as acquired in 1803 through a woman named
"Nonya" Betong, for 390 Spanish dollars — a Peranakan honorific attached to a Burmese
transaction, which tells you how entangled these communities were before George Town had
paved streets.

None of this is a melting pot. That metaphor is wrong for Penang and it flattens the good
part. What Penang produced is a set of parallel, named, still-identifiable culinary
lineages that borrowed hard from each other at the level of *ingredient*, *technique* and
*vocabulary* while keeping their kitchens separate — largely because of the halal boundary,
and partly because the East India Company's kapitan system made the communities legally
and residentially distinct in the first place.

Which is why a Penang noodle map has to be a graph and not a family tree. A tree assumes
each dish has one parent. Almost none of them do.

## A note on what follows, and on confidence

The chapters after this one work through the threads one at a time: the Chinese waves and
their dialect groups, the two creole kitchens, the Indian-Muslim and Arab layer, the Malay
and Javanese layer, and then the mechanisms by which any of it actually moved from one
kitchen to another. The last chapter is a per-dish dossier.

Everything here is tagged for confidence in the accompanying dataset, and the tagging is
not decoration. Malaysian and Singaporean hawker-dish origin stories are, with a handful of
honourable exceptions, oral-tradition history recycled through food blogs. The typical
chain runs: a stall owner tells a journalist a family story → the journalist prints it →
twenty blogs copy the journalist → Wikipedia cites a blog → and thereafter everyone cites
Wikipedia. Almost none of it is corroborated by newspaper archives, business registrations
or oral-history programmes.

So this account tries to be useful about two different things at once: what is probably
true, and how well we know it. Where a beloved story does not survive checking, that is
said plainly, because the collapse is usually more interesting than the story. The Penang
food canon does not need to be protected from scrutiny. It survives it very well.
