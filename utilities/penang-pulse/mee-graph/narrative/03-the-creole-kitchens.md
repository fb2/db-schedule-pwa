# 3. The creole kitchens: Nyonya, and the one everybody forgets

Penang produced not one creole cuisine but two, from the same demographic accident, and only
one of them survived into the hawker canon. The reason the other did not is instructive
enough that it belongs near the front of any account of the island's food.

The accident is simple. Sea voyages were considered unsuitable for women, so early migrant
men — Hokkien traders from Amoy and elsewhere in Fujian, Tamil Muslim traders from the
Coromandel coast, Hadhrami Arabs — arrived without them and married locally. What the
children inherited was a father's language, religion or trade and a **mother's kitchen**.
Every creole cuisine in this region is a Southeast Asian kitchen operating with imported
ingredients and imported prestige, and reading it the other way round — as a Chinese cuisine
with Malay borrowings — gets the direction of causation backwards.

Genetic work on Peranakan Chinese confirms real admixture rather than purely cultural
creolisation: a Chinese-majority ancestry component plus a detectable Malay and Southeast
Asian one. This was not a metaphor.

## Baba-Nyonya, and why Penang is not Malacca

The Malacca Peranakan community is the older one, tracing to the fifteenth- and
sixteenth-century sultanate and continuing under Portuguese and Dutch rule. **The Penang
branch is a later, largely nineteenth-century formation, and it was substantially fed *from*
Malacca** — Baba families relocating north to the new British free port, joining Hokkien
traders arriving directly from Fujian and from Kedah's earlier Chinese settlements. So Penang
Nyonya culture is simultaneously a Malacca offshoot and a fresh northern creolisation, which
is precisely why it diverged rather than replicated.

In the northern ports the local pool was not only Malay. Sources consistently name **Siamese
and Burmese women** alongside Malay women of Acehnese, Javanese, Balinese and Batak descent
as partners of the early seafarers. That is a real ingredient in the flavour profile, not a
demographic footnote.

### Two different creoles, and you can hear it

This is the sharpest Penang/Malacca distinction and it is well established. **Baba Malay** —
the Malacca and Singapore Peranakan vernacular — is a creole with a predominantly *Malay*
lexicon and substantial *Hokkien* grammatical structure, especially in kinship, ritual,
household and food vocabulary. Penang Peranakans did something different: they **kept
Hokkien** as the home language, and loaded it with Malay borrowings instead.

Penang Hokkien's Malay loanwords are dense and entirely everyday: *lô-ti* (bread, from
*roti*), *ko-pi*, *sa-bûn* (soap, from *sabun*), *tu-a-lâ* (towel), *jam-bân* (toilet),
*sâm-pah* (rubbish), *senduk* (ladle). Penang Hokkien even takes English words *via* Malay,
which shows the borrowing route ran through the Malay-speaking street rather than direct from
the colonial administration.

The food consequence is that Penang Chinese hawker vocabulary is Malay-inflected in a way
Singapore's and Malacca's is less so. When a Penang Hokkien stall says *asam*, *belacan*,
*rempah*, *kunyit*, *serai*, *bunga kantan*, *daun kesum*, *taucu*, *petis* — it is not
code-switching. Those words have been fully internalised into a Chinese kitchen. You can
hear the fusion before you taste it.

### The technique

The defining technical act of Nyonya cooking is the **rempah**: an aromatic paste hand-pounded
in a granite *batu lesung*, ingredients added hardest to softest, then fried long in oil
(*tumis*) until it *pecah minyak* — "breaks oil," the point at which the oil separates and the
paste darkens from crimson to maroon.

That single procedure is what separates Nyonya food from mainstream Chinese cooking, which
relies on stock, wok heat, and soy or fermented-bean seasoning rather than a pounded wet
spice base. The other non-negotiable is **belacan** — fermented shrimp paste, toasted before
use — an indigenous Southeast Asian umami source standing in precisely the position that
fermented soybean occupies in Chinese cooking.

If you want one test for whether a dish is Nyonya rather than Chinese, it is not the
ingredient list. It is whether somebody pounded a paste and fried it until it broke.

### Where the two Nyonya cuisines split

| | Malacca / Singapore | **Penang** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fat and body | heavy *santan* for creaminess | coconut used sparingly, and in the signature dish not at all |
| Acid | comparatively disfavoured | liberal — tamarind (*asam jawa*), *asam gelugor* slices, lime, pineapple |
| Aromatics | candlenut, and famously *buah keluak* | **bunga kantan** (torch ginger), **daun kesum**, mint, kaffir lime |
| Emblematic dish | ayam buah keluak | asam laksa |
| Register | subtler, individually legible flavours | sour, fiery, herbal, high top notes |

The conventional explanation is proximity to Siam. That is right as far as it goes, but the
better formulation is the one from Chapter 1: Penang Nyonya cooking absorbed the *Kedah
Malay* sour-fish idiom, which is itself continuous with southern Thai foodways. So the "Thai
influence" is really shared northern-peninsula regional cuisine. There is a lovely piece of
internal evidence for this. Penang cooks call the *coconut* version of laksa "Siamese laksa" —
and since Thai laksa is the coconut one, that label tells you Penang's own self-understanding
places the sour, coconut-free style as the local norm and the creamy style as the imported
other.

Wendy Hutton's observation is the most useful primary-ish testimony available: "a Penang
Nonya will follow Thai cooks and make a thin sour fishy gravy" where a Singapore Nonya uses
santan.

### What the Nyonya kitchen put on the map

**Asam laksa** is the flagship: poached and flaked *ikan kembung* in a broth soured with
tamarind and *asam gelugur*, over a rempah of dried chilli, shallot, lemongrass, galangal and
belacan; thick round rice noodles; and a garnish battery of shredded pineapple, cucumber, raw
onion, mint and sliced bunga kantan, finished with a dark swirl of **hae ko**.

Hae ko deserves its own paragraph. It is a thick, black, molasses-like fermented prawn paste —
a *Chinese-Penang manufactured product*, with Cheong Kim Chuan the George Town institution —
whose Malay name, **petis**, points straight at the Javanese fermented-paste tradition. It is
the ingredient that makes Penang asam laksa taste like Penang rather than like Kedah. And it
turns up in two other places: Penang rojak, and Penang chee cheong fun, where it dresses a
Cantonese steamed rice sheet. One condiment crossing the Chinese/Malay line three times in
one town, in three different dishes, at three different stalls.

**Lam mee** is the quieter and more revealing dish. Yellow noodles under a clear, long-simmered
prawn-and-pork-rib broth poured over at the table, with finely shredded toppings arranged on
top — omelette, chicken, prawn, bean sprouts, long beans, fried shallots — and sambal belacan
on the side, without which Penangites will tell you it is not lam mee. It is a birthday dish,
made for milestone years at sixty, seventy, eighty, offered at prayers and distributed to
relatives with a large red *ang ku kueh*.

What makes it interesting is what it did to its inheritance. The account Penang cooks give is
that Baba-Nyonya descendants of Fujian immigrants brought **lor mee** with them and adjusted
it progressively until it branched off. If that is right — and it is family-tradition history,
not documented — then lam mee kept lor mee's *structure* (noodle, poured liquid, assembled
toppings) and discarded its defining *lor* braise, replacing dark soy and cornstarch with
clear prawn stock. A creole dish is not usually a blend. It is more often a substitution: keep
the architecture, replace the load-bearing element.

One correction while we are here. A widely circulated account holds that "lam mee" *is* loh
mee, lightened for Cantonese palates and re-pronounced. It fails four ways: the broths are
different in kind, not degree; lam mee has a ritual function loh mee lacks; the phonetic story
runs the wrong way geographically, since Penang is not Cantonese-majority; and 淋 *lam*, "to
pour over," is a perfectly good independent etymology for a noodle over which broth is poured.
The graph records this as a `confused_with` edge and an explicit `unevidenced_link` — not as a
genealogy.

Also on the Nyonya list: **lemak laksa**, the coconut sibling, sometimes sold half-and-half in
the same bowl as asam; **curry mee**, whose santan-rempah-daun kesum architecture is
unambiguously Peranakan even though no household invention claim survives scrutiny; **mee
siam**; and **chap chai**, the everyday braise of cabbage, glass noodles, fermented bean paste,
dried lily bud and wood ear — a Chinese Buddhist template reworked with Southeast Asian
seasoning, and the clearest reminder that most Nyonya cooking was never hawker food at all.

## Jawi Peranakan: the creole that left almost no trace

Now the one that gets left out, and it is *specifically Penang*.

**Jawi Peranakan** — the Penang-preferred variant is **Jawi Pekan**, "town-born Jawi" —
denotes locally born, Malay-speaking Muslims of mixed South Asian and Malay descent: the
children of Indian Muslim merchant men, predominantly Tamil and Malabari, and local Malay
Muslim women. "Jawi" is the Arabic term for Southeast Asia and for the Arabic-derived script
used to write Malay; "Peranakan" is Malay for locally born. The community also absorbed
Arab-Malay offspring and later intermarried with Sayyid families and Malay aristocracy,
screening partners for wealth and status rather than ethnic origin — which is why the category
is porous at its edges.

The demographic driver is identical to the Baba-Nyonya one: South Asian women barely migrated
before the 1860s. The difference is that this community was **Muslim**, and therefore
assimilable into Malay identity in a way the Chinese Peranakans were not.

By the later nineteenth century they were an elite — merchants, landlords, English-educated,
heavily represented in colonial clerical service. Their centre was the Indian-Muslim quarter
around **Lebuh Chulia** and **Jalan Masjid Kapitan Keling**. And they financed
***Jawi Peranakkan*** (1876–1895), published weekly on Mondays in Jawi script and generally
identified as the **first Malay-language newspaper**. Its first editor was Munshi Mohamed
Said bin Dada Mohyiddin, Penang-born of Tamil Jawi Peranakan parentage, who held the post
until his death in 1888.

Then they vanished, for three converging reasons. The Great Depression bankrupted many Jawi
Peranakan mercantile houses and collapsed their rents. Other mercantile groups displaced them.
And from the turn of the twentieth century the political incentive ran strongly toward
registering simply as **Malay**, since Malays were being positioned as indigenous successors
to the British. The Jawi Peranakan also tended to religious reformism, challenged Malay royal
authority in religious matters, and mostly had never been subjects of a sultan — so they
lacked the attributes then being written into "true Malay" identity. Most descendants today
register as Malay. Munshi Abdullah was one; so is Mahathir Mohamad.

### The food, and why we barely have it

This is thinly documented, and honesty requires saying so: it is reconstructed through a
handful of surviving family kitchens and restaurants, and the main source is a well-researched
but non-peer-reviewed heritage site writing in a confident essayistic register. Treat the
dish-level claims as low to medium confidence. In the dataset they are flagged.

The consistent description is **Malay dish architecture with Indian and Middle Eastern spice
and enrichment**, and the reported markers are specific enough to be worth recording:

- **Ghee** (*minyak sapi*) as the primary cooking fat in place of coconut or palm oil — an
  Indo-Persian rather than Malay choice.
- **Nuts and seeds as thickeners** — ground almond, cashew, poppy seed — producing smooth,
  non-oily gravies.
- **Perfumes**: rose water, saffron, dried fruit.
- **The "four friends" sweet-spice quartet**: cinnamon, clove, star anise, cardamom.
- Dishes named across sources: *nasi tomato* as the everyday flagship; *nasi lemuni*, rice
  cooked with lemuni leaf, dark and herbaceous, with post-partum warming associations;
  *Jawi bamieh*, lamb braised with okra in tomato — a direct Hadhrami and Levantine *bamia*
  descendant; a sealed-pot *dam* Straits briyani; *bubur Asyura*, a communal porridge of ten
  to twenty ingredients for Muharram.
- Rich mains paired deliberately with astringent **ulam** — raw foraged leaves, wild ginger
  bud, sour carambola — and closed with rose sherbet, basil seeds and malva nut.

The load-bearing point is structural, and it is solid even where the recipes are not.
**Jawi Peranakan cuisine is a distinct third creole**, alongside Nyonya and Mamak: Muslim and
Malay-lexicon where Nyonya is Chinese, and far richer and more Middle Eastern than mamak
street food. And it was **elite and celebratory rather than hawker** — which is exactly why it
left almost no mark on the hawker canon and is nearly extinct today.

That is the lesson worth carrying. Culinary survival in Penang did not go to the most
sophisticated cuisine. It went to the cuisines that could be sold from a cart at seven in the
morning for the price of a coffee. Nyonya cooking survives in the hawker canon only where it
was *commercialisable* — asam laksa, curry mee — and its home dishes, lam mee included, are
widely and correctly described as better at home than at a stall. Jawi Peranakan cooking never
made that transition at all.

There is a whole missing hawker cuisine here that Penang simply never had. Worth remembering
next time somebody calls the island's food scene comprehensive.
