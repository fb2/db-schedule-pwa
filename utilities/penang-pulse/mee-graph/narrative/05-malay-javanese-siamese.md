# 5. Malay, Javanese, Siamese: the substrate and the two arrivals

The Malay thread in Penang food is the hardest to write about, and not because there is less of
it. There is less *written* about it. Every Chinese dish in this dataset has at least a named
founder story; mee udang at Sungai Dua — a substantial, decades-old commercial cluster that
people drive across a bridge for — has none at all. Food media treat it as a destination without
a history.

That is a bias in the *sources*, not in the dishes, and a graph built naively on available
sources will inherit it. It is recorded in the dataset as `x-evidence-asymmetry` precisely so it
can be corrected for rather than quietly reproduced.

## The substrate: Kedah

The base layer is Kedah Malay, and Chapter 1 made the case. What it means at the level of the
plate: freshwater and inshore fish; rice as the organising staple; tamarind and dried *asam
gelugur* as the souring agents rather than lime alone; belacan as the umami anchor; sambal
pounded from dried chilli; and a herbal register — *daun kesum*, *bunga kantan*, mint, kaffir
lime — that Penang's Nyonya kitchens borrowed wholesale.

**Laksa Kedah** — also *laksa utara*, northern laksa — is the clearest expression, and it is
worth setting against Penang asam laksa carefully, because the differences are small,
consistent, and diagnostic:

| | Penang asam laksa | Laksa Kedah / utara |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fish | *ikan kembung*, poached then **flaked into strands** | kembung or selayang, **pounded to a paste into the broth** |
| Souring | tamarind pulp, plus gelugur | dried **asam keping** slices predominant |
| Chilli | fresh red | dried |
| Garnish | cucumber, onion, mint, **pineapple**, **bunga kantan**, **hae ko** | belacan, **daun selom**, often **sambal kelapa** |
| Texture | thinner, fish visible in strands | thicker, smoother, fish fully integrated |
| Boundary | contains hae ko — a Chinese-manufactured product | **halal by default**, no hae ko |

This is a **Malay** dish, not a Peranakan one. And the direction of travel between the two is
genuinely unresolved. Penang was part of Kedah until 1786 and the island's Malay substrate *is*
Kedahan, so it is at least as plausible that Penang asam laksa is a Peranakan-Chinese
elaboration of an existing Kedah sour fish laksa — adding hae ko, pineapple and Nyonya
knife-work — as that the influence ran the other way. English-language sources overwhelmingly
narrate Penang as origin and Kedah as variant. That is almost certainly an artefact of Penang's
tourism prominence rather than evidence, and the dataset encodes the uncertainty in both
directions rather than picking a side.

Also from this layer: **mee udang**, whole unpeeled prawns in a tomato-and-chilli gravy with
tamarind, ordered *rebus* or *goreng*. Timothy Tye describes it as "primarily a Malay dish
similar to the Jawa Mee sold by Chinese hawkers" — which is a precise and useful observation,
because it puts mee udang inside the same tomato-gravy family as mee jawa and mee rebus rather
than in a category of its own. Two clusters: **Teluk Kumbar** on the island's southwest coast,
which has become a style name used by stalls all over Penang the way "Penang Road famous" works
for cendol, and **Sungai Dua** on the mainland near the Muda estuary and its prawn fishery. The
geography is self-explaining: the fishery is there.

## The Javanese arrival

Beyond the Kedah substrate, Penang and Province Wellesley received substantial migration from
the Netherlands Indies: **Javanese** as the largest stream, much of it estate labour and, in the
early twentieth century, indentured *kuli kontrak*; plus **Minangkabau**, **Bugis**, **Banjar**,
**Bawean**, **Mandailing** and **Acehnese**. Post-independence Malaysian ethnic classification
folded all of them into "Malay," which erased the culinary genealogies from official view even
as the dish names preserved them.

The Javanese thread is the one that reached Penang as **whole dishes** rather than only as
ingredients or techniques — which distinguishes it from, say, *petis* and *taucu*, which arrived
as components and dissolved into other people's cooking.

### Mee soto, and a nice recursion

**Mee soto** is Javanese *soto ayam* transplanted onto noodles: a clear but deeply aromatic
turmeric-yellow chicken broth with lemongrass, galangal, garlic, shallot, candlenut, coriander
seed and cumin; shredded poached chicken, bean sprouts, Chinese celery, fried shallots; and
near-obligatory **begedil**, the mashed-potato-and-beef fritter, which is itself a Dutch
*frikadel* loan into Indonesian. Sambal kicap on the side.

*Soto* itself is argued — following Denys Lombard — to derive from a Chinese spiced-offal-soup
term, acculturated in nineteenth-century Java with turmeric and local aromatics into the yellow
herbal broth we know. Soto's early vendors in Java were often Chinese. Which gives the pleasing
recursion: **soto is already a Sino-Javanese creole dish before it becomes a Malay dish in
Penang**, and then acquires a Dutch fritter on the way. Three empires and two diasporas in one
bowl, none of them announced on the signboard.

Regional Indonesian variants — soto Lamongan, soto Madura — are the direct ancestors, carried by
Javanese and Madurese migrants, probably in the early 1900s. And the same dish reached Suriname
with Javanese indentured labourers, where it is *saoto*. A Penang mamak stall has a cousin in
South America.

### Mee rebus and mee jawa: the most confused pair in Malaysian food

These two get conflated constantly, so it is worth being exact.

**What they share:** blanched yellow alkaline wheat noodle; a thick gravy **thickened with
mashed sweet potato**; ground peanut; a garnish set of bean sprouts, hard-boiled egg, fried
beancurd and fried shallots; a lime wedge; sambal or sliced chilli. Both are breakfast or tea
food. Both are, structurally, "cheap noodle made substantial by a starchy sweet gravy."

**What differs:**

| | Mee rebus (Malay / Indian-Muslim) | Penang mee jawa (Chinese) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Kitchen | **halal** | often **pork-rib stock** — non-halal |
| Umami base | **taucu**, grago shrimp, mutton or beef | shrimp or pork stock, **tomato paste** |
| Spicing | curry powder, turmeric, lemongrass, ginger — actively spiced | **minimal** — little or no turmeric, fennel, curry powder or rempah |
| Sweet/sour | palm sugar and calamansi | tomato tartness against sweet-potato sweetness; sweeter overall |
| Fritters | usually none | **prawn fritters and crisp shards integral** |
| Colour | brown-orange, turmeric-tinged | reddish-brown from tomato |

Note the small elegant detail: **Penang's Chinese mee jawa omits taucu** — the fermented soybean
paste that is Chinese in origin — precisely because the Malay version had already made it the
signature. When a dish crosses a community boundary, the receiving kitchen sometimes drops the
ingredient it could most easily have kept, because that ingredient now belongs to somebody
else. NLB adds a wonderfully unromantic corroborating detail: the Penang mee jawa gravy is
flavoured with a can of tomato soup.

Mee rebus has four live origin theories and none is settled: derived from *mie jawa*, though NLB
notes the Javanese dish "bears little resemblance to mee rebus in its current form"; carried
south from the northern states by Indian Muslim peddlers on kandar poles, which is NLB's own
committed opening line and the strongest single attribution; created in Singapore before WWII,
per Khir Johari; or a fifteenth-to-seventeenth-century Penang Peranakan dish, which is
implausible on its face since alkaline yellow wheat noodles were not a mass commodity in the
peninsula then.

Mee jawa has three: Chinese-Javanese Peranakans migrating from Medan and Malacca; direct
Javanese or Minangkabau settlers; or a local Penang Chinese invention wearing an exotic name.
Malay Mail's assessment is the honest default and this dataset adopts it — "the origin of this
hawker fare is unclear but its name suggests that it is linked back to the heydays of the Java
Peranakan as its varied ingredients suggest different cultures."

**The right model for the pair:** siblings with a bidirectional influence edge, not parent and
child. Two hawker solutions to the same economic problem, converged on the same starch and
garnish set, arrived at from **opposite sides of the halal line**, and then borrowing from each
other for a century on shared streets. NLB treats mee jawa as a variant of mee rebus; Penang
treats them as separate dishes. Both are defensible, which is usually the sign that the
question has been posed wrongly.

One more transmission mechanism, hiding in plain sight: NLB records that mee rebus stalls
"usually also offer mee goreng" because the ingredients overlap. **Co-sold at the same stall** is
not a footnote. It is one of the most productive ways recipes actually move, and the dataset
carries it as its own edge type.

Also from the Javanese layer: **mee jawa's cousin mee bandung** from Muar — beef stock, prawns,
chilli, sweet-potato thickening — and **lontong**, compressed rice cooked in a banana-leaf
cylinder (as against *ketupat*, rhombic, in woven palm), served in Penang with *sayur lodeh*,
sambal, serunding, boiled egg and *kuah kacang*, strongly associated with Hari Raya. Javanese
tradition attributes the spread of lontong and ketupat eating to the Islamisation of Java and
credits Sunan Kalijaga with introducing it, which is legend rather than history. Malaysia and
Indonesia both claim it; the honest answer is that it is Javanese food naturalised in the
peninsula.

## The Siamese and Burmese layer, and what it actually contributed

Siamese suzerainty over Kedah, and Siamese and Burmese pressure on it, are the reason Penang
exists as a British possession at all. Malaysian **Siamese** — Thai Malaysians — are concentrated
in the former tributary states of Kedah, Perlis, Kelantan and Terengganu, with a long-settled
Penang community. Two adjacent institutions in Pulau Tikus, facing each other across the lane,
are the physical proof: **Wat Chayamangkalaram** (1845), the oldest Siamese temple in Penang, and
the **Dhammikarama Burmese Buddhist Temple**, founded in 1803 and the oldest Buddhist temple in
traditional Burmese style outside Myanmar.

The food footprint is best described as **atmospheric and ingredient-level rather than
dish-level**, and this is where a lot of writing overreaches.

**What the Siamese layer genuinely contributed:** the sour-and-hot axis. Tamarind, asam gelugur,
lime, torch ginger, kaffir lime leaf, mint, galangal — the register shared with southern Thai
cooking and central to Penang Nyonya food. Framed correctly, as Chapter 1 argued, this is not
borrowing across a border; it is one northern-peninsula regional cuisine, and Kuroda's work on
the Tai-speaking Muslim **Samsam** of inland Kedah shows how gradual that boundary really was.

**What it did not contribute, whatever the name says:** **mee siam** carries the reference in its
name without being Thai at all — there is no such dish in Thai cuisine. Khir Johari's reading is
the most economical: the name refers to the **Thai-imported dried vermicelli**, not to the dish,
exactly as *laksa* originally named a noodle rather than a soup. The alternative reading is that
it names a flavour register that Malayan eaters heard as Thai-tasting. Both may be partly right;
neither makes the dish Thai. Note also the late documentary attestation — the first Singapore
newspaper mention is 1950, hawkers at the Esplanade selling to evening strollers.

And Penang's own **laksa lemak** is sometimes locally called "Siamese laksa," which as noted in
Chapter 3 is evidence about Penang's self-image rather than about Thailand.

**What arrived recently and should not be back-dated:** **tom yum noodles** and **Thai boat
noodles** are a late twentieth- and twenty-first-century arrival into Penang hawker culture, via
Thai restaurants, Thai-Malaysian cooks, and cross-border traffic through Bukit Kayu Hitam and
Padang Besar. They are now thoroughly naturalised — Raja Uda and Butterworth on the mainland are
a recognised tom yum cluster, and the Penang style is a thick, red, chilli-paste-heavy broth with
a heavy hand of lime and bunga kantan, distinguished by local writers from the clear, tangy,
herb-forward Thai style. Customers select their own ingredients from a tray, which is a Malaysian
hawker convention rather than a Thai one. Ghee Lian on Perak Road opened in 2012 with a *green*
tom yum noodle: local invention inside an imported category, which is Penang doing what Penang
does, seventy years after the last time it did it.

But it is a commercial diffusion, not a heritage layer, and conflating the two makes the older
Siamese substrate invisible.

**And one claim to refuse outright.** *Mohinga*, the Burmese fish-and-rice-noodle breakfast soup,
is conceptually a sibling of laksa utara — fish broth, rice noodles, sour and herbal garnish —
and Penang has had a Burmese community since the early 1800s, in the very neighbourhood where
several of this series' episodes were eaten. It is a tempting connection. **No source establishes
transmission in either direction.** The dataset carries it as an explicit `unevidenced_link`,
which is to say: recorded as a non-link, so that nobody has to rediscover the absence later.

Negative results are results. A graph that only contains the connections people believe in is
just a rumour with better typography.
