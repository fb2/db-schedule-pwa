# Dish Dossiers — Part B
## Research dossier 04 — the wheat-and-rice noodle families around Penang: Hakka, Teochew, Cantonese, Sabahan, Thai and the naming-collision problems

Scope: twenty-two dish entries covering the noodle families that either (a) are present in Penang but are *not* Penang-origin dishes, (b) are Malaysian dishes Penang eaters constantly compare themselves against, or (c) are Penang dishes missing from the standard checklist. Companion to dossier 01 (Chinese dialect groups), dossier 02 (Malay/Nyonya/Mamak/Arab threads) and dossier 03 (dishes, part A).

Confidence labels used throughout: **high** (multiple independent, reputable sources agree, or documentary evidence exists), **medium** (plausible, consistently reported, but sourced mainly to food media rather than archival or scholarly work), **low** (single weak source, or inference), **disputed** (sources actively conflict).

A methodological warning that applies to nearly every entry below. Malaysian and Singaporean hawker-dish origin stories are almost entirely **oral-tradition history recycled through food blogs**. The typical chain is: a stall owner tells a journalist a family story → the journalist prints it → twenty blogs copy the journalist → Wikipedia cites a blog → everyone thereafter cites Wikipedia. Almost none of it is corroborated by newspaper archives, business registrations, or oral-history programmes. Where a claim has *only* this provenance I say so explicitly. Two claims in this document have better-than-usual documentation (Lanzhou/Ma Baozi; the Singapore bak chor mee "Hunchback" lineage, which has living descendants and a continuous supply chain). Most do not.

## 1. Pan Mee / Ban Mian (板麵)

**Names.** Mandarin *bǎnmiàn* 板麵 / 板面. Hokkien *pán-mī*, which is what the Malay/English spelling "pan mee" transliterates — "pan mee" is effectively a Malay-orthography rendering of the Hokkien reading, not of the Mandarin [S1]. Hakka names recorded: *man-foon-cha-guo* (麵粉茶粿) and *dao-ma-chet* (刀嬤切, roughly "knife-cut") [S1]. Hokkien alternative name **mee hoon kueh** (麵粉粿, *mī-hún-kué*, literally "wheat-flour kueh/snack") [S1]. Cantonese speakers in the Klang Valley will say *baan min*. Malay: *mi kepingan* is occasionally seen but in practice Malaysians of all races say "pan mee". No Tamil name in circulation.

**Etymology.** 板 *ban/pan* = "board, plank, block". The Wikipedia-transmitted explanation is that the name comes from the Hakka technique of cutting dough into straight strands using a **wooden block as a straight-edge**, i.e. "board noodle" [S1]. A competing gloss — that 板 refers to the flat *plank-like shape* of the noodle — is at least as plausible and is what most Malaysian speakers assume. Confidence: **medium** for the ruler explanation (it appears in one lineage of sources and propagates); **high** that the character means board/plank.

**Noodle type.** Fresh **hand-formed non-alkaline wheat noodle**, water/flour/salt, often with egg. Three distinct physical forms are sold, frequently at the same stall, and Malaysians treat them as one dish family [S1][S7]:
1. **Mee hoon kueh** — dough rolled flat and *torn* by hand into irregular 2–5 cm bite-sized pieces (the Hokkien method).
2. **Pan mee "thick"** — flat cut strips roughly 1 cm wide.
3. **Pan mee "thin"** / **you mee** — round or near-round strands of roughly spaghetti gauge (see §15).
Modern stalls almost universally use a pasta-roller/cutter for 2 and 3, hand-tearing only for 1 [S1][S7].

**Cultural origin.** **Hakka**, from Guangdong (the Malaysian Hakka population derives substantially from Huizhou, Jiaying/Meixian, Dabu and Hepo prefectures), with a **Hokkien** parallel tradition. Wikipedia's formulation is the useful one: what is sold today is a *hybrid* — the Hakka shaved/cut dough off a block, the Hokkien rolled and tore it, and the modern hawker offering merges both under the Hakka name [S1]. The anchovy (*ikan bilis*) stock base is the Southeast Asian layer: Chinese-mainland ban mian uses pork or plain water stock; the Malaysian dish is built on **dried anchovy**, a Malay-archipelago pantry staple, plus dried shiitake, minced pork, fried shallot, and a leafy green [S1][S5][S108]. That anchovy base is the single clearest marker that pan mee is a *Malaysian* dish rather than a transplanted Chinese one. Confidence: **high**.

**Signature elements.** Ikan bilis stock and fried crispy ikan bilis garnish; minced pork; dried shiitake; sweet potato leaves (*daun keledek*) or **sayur manis / mani cai** (*Sauropus androgynus*) [S1]; a poached or dropped egg; sambal on the side. Vinegar, sometimes with sugar, is added to balance [S1].

**Penang-specific variation.** Pan mee is **not a Penang dish**. Penang's Chinese population is Hokkien-dominant with a Teochew and Cantonese minority and a comparatively small Hakka presence; pan mee's centre of gravity is the **Klang Valley, Selangor and the Hakka tin-belt towns** (Ampang, Serdang, Rawang, Kepong, Seremban, Ipoh). Where pan mee appears in Penang it is a late-20th-century import, generally under the Klang Valley "chilli pan mee" branding rather than the older soup form. Confidence: **high** for the geography; **medium** for the timing of its arrival in Penang, which is not documented.

**Chilli pan mee — the documented origin.** This is one of the better-attested Malaysian invention claims, though still thin. Restoran **Kin Kin** (建記), founded **1985** by hawker **Tan Kok Hong**, originally at a stall under a tree in **Chow Kit**, Kuala Lumpur, now at 40 Jalan Dewan Sultan Sulaiman, Kampung Baru [S2][S3][S4]. Tan's account, given to The Rakyat Post: he sold conventional pan mee in clear pork broth, noticed customers spooning chilli into their bowls, and built a dry version around a proprietary dried-chilli condiment; the runny poached egg was added later for texture [S2][S3]. The finished dish is dry noodles (spaghetti gauge, not torn) with minced pork, fried shallot, crisp anchovies, a poached egg stirred through, and the dry chilli-flake condiment, which Tan does not sell separately and guards [S2].

Confidence: **medium-high** that Kin Kin originated the dish; **low** on any specific detail. The evidence base is Tan's own testimony repeated across food media over three decades, with no competing claimant of comparable standing. Wikipedia's citation for "invented in Chow Kit" is a 2017 food blog [S1] — which is exactly the citation-laundering pattern flagged above. The 1985 date is consistent across sources (a 2023 social post describes a "39-year tradition", implying 1984–85) [S2][S3][S4]. **Flag:** the frequent blog assertion that chilli pan mee is "a traditional Hakka dish" is false — it is a documented 1980s KL invention on a Hakka substrate.

**Documented relationships.** Parent: Hakka knife-cut/torn noodle traditions of Guangdong. Sibling: **mee hoon kueh** (same dough, different forming method — the two are best modelled as *variants of one dish*, not separate dishes). Descendant: **chilli pan mee** (1985). Cousins by technique: Chinese *dāoxiāomiàn* (刀削麵, Shanxi knife-shaved noodle) shares the shaving-off-a-block method but has no historical connection. Sabah's Hakka noodle culture (§2–3) is a parallel, not derived, branch.

## 2. Sabah Pan Mee — is it actually distinct?

**Short answer: no, not as a named dish — but Sabah's Hakka noodle culture is genuinely distinct, and that is why the question keeps being asked.** Confidence: **medium-high**.

**The demographic fact.** Sabah is the one Malaysian state where the Chinese population is **Hakka-majority** rather than Hokkien-, Cantonese- or Teochew-majority [S8][S11]. Sabah's Hakka arrived largely through late-19th and early-20th-century British North Borneo Chartered Company recruitment, including a strong **Basel Mission Christian Hakka** stream — a different migration mechanism from the clan/kongsi chain migration that populated the peninsula. The consequence, reported by CNA and Daily Express, is that Sabah's noodle map is organised **by district rather than by dialect**: Tuaran mee, Beaufort mee, Tenom mee, Tamparuli mee, Kota Belud soup mee, each a locally-evolved Hakka noodle [S8][S11][S12].

**What Sabah does not have** is a distinctly-named "Sabah pan mee". Searches surface no dish by that name. What people who use the phrase usually mean is one of:
- **Sang nyuk mee** (生肉麵, "fresh meat noodle") — Sabah's actual signature pork noodle, yellow egg noodle or bee hoon, served either in a peppery clear pork broth or as *kon lau* (乾撈, dry-tossed) in dark soy and rendered **pork-lard oil** with lard croutons and the soup on the side. Reported as invented in **1979 by two Chinese brothers in Tawau**, on Sabah's east coast [S13]. This is the "fried pork dry version" the brief gestures at. Confidence: **medium** (single-lineage claim, no archival corroboration).
- **Tuaran mee** (§3).
- Ordinary pan mee made in Sabah with **sayur manis** rather than sweet potato leaves.

**The sayur manis link — and why it matters.** *Sauropus androgynus*, known as **sayur manis** ("sweet vegetable"), **cekur manis**, **mani cai / manicai** (马尼菜), **cangkuk manis**, and in peninsular marketing as "**Sabah vegetable**", is the leafy green most associated with Bornean Chinese cooking and is one of the two standard greens in pan mee [S1][S14][S15]. It is not a Chinese vegetable; it is a Southeast Asian one, and its adoption into a Hakka wheat-noodle soup is a clean example of substrate ingredient displacing homeland ingredient.

The plant carries a **documented toxicity risk**: an epidemic of **bronchiolitis obliterans** (irreversible obstructive lung disease) occurred in Taiwan in 1994–95 among people consuming large quantities of raw or juiced *S. androgynus*; it must be cooked and should not be eaten raw in quantity [S14]. Confidence: **high** — this is in the medical literature, unlike almost everything else in this document. It is worth stating because food writing about "Sabah veg" almost never mentions it.

**Relationships.** Sabah's Hakka noodles and peninsular pan mee are **cousins descended from a common Hakka parent**, not parent-and-child. The peninsula's Hakka expression is the torn/cut dough noodle in anchovy broth; Sabah's is the district-named fried or soup egg noodle. The unifying features are Hakka provenance, pork, lard, and dark soy.

## 3. Tuaran Mee (斗亚兰面)

**Names.** Chinese 斗亚兰面, Mandarin *Dòuyàlán miàn*; Hakka rendering recorded as *tao-ah-lan men* / *diu ah lan men*; Malay **mi Tuaran** [S8]. English: Tuaran noodle, Tuaran fried noodle.

**Etymology.** Purely toponymic: Tuaran is a town on Sabah's west coast, north of Kota Kinabalu. The name is a *retronym* — Wikipedia records that until the mid-1980s Sabahan Hakka simply called fried noodles *chao men* (炒麵); as the Tuaran style spread, Hakka elsewhere in Sabah began specifying "tao-ah-lan men" [S8][S9]. Confidence: **medium-high** (sourced to a Guardian street-food piece and Malaysian food press).

**Noodle type.** A **handmade egg noodle**, thin and springy, made specifically for this dish. The defining feature is the **three-stage cooking**: the noodles are (1) deep- or shallow-fried until crisp, (2) briefly boiled to rehydrate, then (3) stir-fried with egg, vegetables and meat [S8][S10]. The egg coats the strands and produces the characteristic golden, glossy finish. This is technically closer to the *yi mein* logic (fry-then-rehydrate, §11) than to ordinary chow mein.

**Cultural origin.** **Hakka**, Sabah. Created in **1952 by a "Madam Si"**, proliferating among Sabah's Hakka communities in Tuaran through the **late 1970s**, when it displaced the older **knife-cut noodle** as the local standard [S8][S9]. Confidence: **medium** — the 1952/Madam Si attribution rests on the Guardian piece and Malaysian food media; no primary documentation. Note the structurally important part of the claim, which is better-supported than the personal attribution: Tuaran mee **replaced knife-cut noodles**, i.e. Sabah's Hakka noodle culture shifted from a hand-cut wheat-dough tradition (the pan mee family) to a fried egg-noodle tradition within living memory.

**Key ingredients.** Char siu slices, choy sum, egg, sometimes prawn; served with **chun kien**, a local Hakka spring roll, and occasionally with **lihing** (Kadazan-Dusun rice wine) worked into the dish or served alongside — a direct indigenous-Sabahan borrowing with no peninsular equivalent [S8][S12].

**Penang relevance.** None directly; Tuaran mee is on this map as the Hakka fried-noodle pole against which peninsular Hakka mee (§20) and KL Hokkien char (§17) can be compared. Confidence: **high**.

**Relationships.** Sibling to Beaufort mee, Tenom mee, Tamparuli mee (the district-noodle set) [S11][S12]. Technical cousin to yi mein (§11) via the fry-then-boil method. Descendant of Hakka *chao men*.

## 4. Bak Chor Mee (肉脞麵)

**Names.** 肉脞麵 / 肉脞面. Hokkien POJ *bah-chhò-mī*; Teochew *neg8 co3 mi7*; Mandarin *ròucuǒ miàn* [S18]. English: minced meat/minced pork noodles. The character 脞 (*cuǒ*, "minced, finely chopped") is rare enough that many stalls write 肉挫 or 肉锉 instead.

**Etymology.** Literally "minced-meat noodle". Straightforward and undisputed [S18].

**Noodle type.** Diner's choice, and the choice is the dish's principal axis of variation: **mee pok** (flat yellow egg-and-lye noodle) or **mee kia** (thin round egg noodle) — see §5. Rice vermicelli and *mee tai bak* are offered at some stalls. Fresh alkaline egg noodle in both cases [S18].

**Cultural origin.** **Teochew**, Chaoshan (Chaozhou/Shantou/Jieyang, eastern Guangdong), transmitted via Singapore. The Chaoshan ancestor is 潮汕干面 — a soup or dry noodle with minced meat, offal and dumplings [S20]. The most specific documented lineage: **陈联福** left **Zhao'an county, Fujian** in the late 1910s, learned the noodle trade in **Chaozhou city, Guangdong**, and emigrated to Singapore in the **1920s**, settling in **Chai Chee** in the east of the island. He hawked from a shoulder-pole two-basket stall following wayang (street-opera) crowds; the load bent his back, and his stall became known as **驼背面, "Hunchback Noodle"** [S17]. Johor Kaki's reporting notes that his version is found **neither in Zhao'an nor Chaozhou nor in Johor Bahru** — i.e. it is a Singapore creation on a Chaoshan template. Eleven stalls run by his descendants and former staff still operate; the noodle recipe passed to Lau Boon Heng Food Manufacturing in Chai Chee, which still supplies all eleven [S17].

Confidence: **medium-high** — unusually good for this genre, with a named individual, a traceable descendant network, and a continuous supplier. Still not archivally verified.

Note the Zhao'an detail, which is analytically important: Zhao'an is a **Fujian county on the Guangdong border** whose speech is Hokkien-Teochew transitional. The dish's name is Hokkien (*bah-chhò-mī*) while its seasoning is Teochew (fish sauce, black vinegar) [S17]. Bak chor mee sits *on* the Hokkien/Teochew boundary rather than cleanly inside one tradition.

**Signature elements.** Blanched minced pork; pork slices; pork liver; braised sliced shiitake; meatballs or fish balls; wonton; *bak pok* (deep-fried lard croutons); fried garlic; scallion; coriander; **Teochew fish sauce**; and in the dry version a sauce of **chilli paste (typically with dried shrimp), lard, soy sauce and Chinese black vinegar** [S17][S18]. The chilli-plus-black-vinegar axis is the identifying signature. A **dry version emerged in the 1950s** and is now dominant over the soup version [S17].

**Why it is rare in Penang.** Three reasons, in descending order of confidence:
1. **Demography.** Penang's Chinese community is Hokkien-dominant; the Teochew presence is real (concentrated historically around Kimberley Street, called *Swatow Kay*, "Swatow Street", in Penang Hokkien) but much smaller than Singapore's [S23]. Singapore's Teochew community is the second-largest dialect group and had the density to make a Teochew hawker dish a national dish. Confidence: **high**.
2. **Niche occupancy.** Penang's Teochew hawkers occupied the *rice-noodle* niche — **koay teow th'ng** (§6) and **koay chiap** (§10) — rather than the wheat-noodle niche. The Penang Teochew expression of "clear pork/duck broth plus poached toppings" is koay teow th'ng, which is functionally bak chor mee's soup cousin with rice noodle substituted. Confidence: **medium-high**.
3. **Condiment culture.** The chilli-and-black-vinegar dressing is a Singapore development of the 1950s [S17], postdating the period when Penang and Singapore hawker repertoires were most freely exchanged.

**Relationships.** Parent: Chaoshan 潮汕干面. Sibling: fish-ball mee pok (*yu wan mee*), sold by the same stalls [S18]. Penang functional analogue: koay teow th'ng. Thai relative: *bamee haeng* (dry egg noodle with minced pork), same Teochew source (§6).

## 5. Mee Pok vs Mee Kia — noodle terminology

This is a terminology entry, not a dish.

**Mee pok** — 麵薄 / 面薄, Hokkien/Teochew *mī-po̍h*, literally "**thin noodle**" in the sense of *shallow in cross-section* — the noodle is a flat ribbon, thin in depth but broad in width, roughly 4–6 mm wide [S18]. Wheat flour, egg, **lye water** (alkaline salts). Chaoshan origin; standard in Singapore, Malaysia and Thailand wherever there is a Teochew hawker population [S18]. The name is a persistent source of confusion for English speakers because "thin" here means *flat*, and mee pok is the *wider* of the pair.

**Mee kia** — 麵仔, Hokkien/Teochew *mī-kiáⁿ*, literally "**child noodle**" or "little noodle"; 仔 *kia* is the Hokkien/Teochew diminutive [S18]. Same dough (wheat, egg, lye), but drawn thin and **round** in section — essentially wonton-noodle gauge. Wikipedia explicitly equates mee kia with **wonton-style noodles / youmian** [S18].

**Youmian** — 幼麵, Cantonese *yau min*, "fine noodle": the Cantonese name for the same thin round egg noodle, used across Hong Kong, Guangdong, Malaysia and Singapore, generally made with whole egg (*cyun daan min*, 全蛋麵) [S21]. So mee kia (Hokkien/Teochew) and yau min / you mee (Cantonese) denote **the same physical noodle** through two dialect lexicons. This matters for §15.

**Related terms in the same family:** *mee tai bak* (short tapered rice "silver needle" noodle); *mee sua* (麵線, wheat thread, §19); *tah* (乾, dry/tossed) vs *terng* (湯, soup) as the ordering axis [S18].

Confidence: **high** on all the above; these are well-documented, stable trade terms.

## 6. Duck Noodle / Duck Koay Teow (鴨肉粿條)

**Names.** Penang: **koay teow th'ng** (粿條湯, *kóe-tiâu-thng*, "kway teow soup"), with the duck version specified as **duck meat koay teow th'ng** / 鴨肉粿條湯 [S22][S23]. Cantonese equivalent noodle: *hor fun* 河粉. Malay: *kuetiau sup itik*. The braised-duck component: **lou ark / lor ark** 卤鸭 (Teochew), *lou ap* (Cantonese). Thai relatives: **kuay tiew ped** (ก๋วยเตี๋ยวเป็ด, rice-noodle duck) and **bamee ped** (บะหมี่เป็ด, egg-noodle duck) [S25].

**Etymology.** 粿條 *kóe-tiâu* = "kueh strips" — rice-flour sheet cut into strips; 湯 *th'ng* = soup. 鴨肉 = duck meat. Note that Thai **บะหมี่ *bamee*** is a direct loan of Teochew *bah-mī* — the Thai word for egg noodle is a Chinese dialect word, which fixes the direction of transmission [S25].

**Noodle type.** Flat **rice** koay teow (silky, non-alkaline, rice-flour sheet cut into ribbons) in the Penang and Chaoshan versions. Thai versions offer the full *sen yai / sen lek / sen mee / bamee* range, with *bamee ped* using wheat-egg noodle [S25].

**Cultural origin.** **Teochew**, Chaoshan. Braising in a *lou* (卤) master-stock of dark soy, galangal, star anise, cinnamon/cassia and five-spice is the signature Teochew technique, and **duck is the Teochew meat of choice** [S22][S24]. In Penang the Teochew concentration around **Kimberley Street ("Swatow Kay")** is the historical anchor [S23]. The best-documented Penang lineage: **Lum Lai Duck Meat Koay Teow Th'ng**, founded in the **late 1970s** by **Lau Lum Lai**, an itinerant pushcart hawker in George Town, now at Cecil Street Market and a Michelin Bib Gourmand [S22]. Confidence: **high** on the Teochew attribution; **medium** on the Lum Lai founding date (business-sourced).

**Signature elements.** A **clear** broth — this is the Teochew aesthetic, "preserve the original flavour of the ingredient" — simmered for hours on duck and/or pork bones with **dried flounder** (*ti poh* 地魚), the northern-Malaysian umami secret weapon, plus herbs [S22][S23]. Toppings: poached or braised duck slices, duck offal, fish balls (sometimes eel-meat fish balls in Penang), sliced fish cake, minced pork, pork slices, fried shallot, spring onion, celery leaf, white pepper. A dish of chilli-and-garlic in light soy on the side.

**Penang-specific variation.** Penang runs **three parallel variants** — duck, pork, and chicken — with duck the prestige option [S22][S23]. Penang broth is **clear**, whereas the Teochew braise itself (lou ark) is dark; the Penang dish separates the two, keeping the soup clear and the meat braised. Compare Singapore, where braised duck is more often served over rice or with the *braising liquid* as the soup base, producing a dark bowl [S24]. Penang also uses **fish balls and fishcake** far more heavily than Chaoshan practice.

**Thai comparison.** *Bamee ped tun* (braised duck egg noodle) uses the **same five-spice braise** and the same Teochew technique, arriving in Thailand with 19th–early-20th-century Teochew migration [S25]. The Thai version characteristically adds a sweeter braise and the table condiment set (sugar, chilli flake, fish sauce, chilli vinegar) that Thai noodle culture applies universally. Penang and Bangkok duck noodles are therefore **siblings, not ancestor and descendant** — both are provincial Teochew braises adapted locally. Confidence: **high**.

**Relationships.** Parent: Chaoshan lou ark plus Chaoshan kway teow soup. Sibling: koay chiap (§10) — same braise, different starch. Sibling: Thai bamee ped / kuay tiew ped. Functional analogue: bak chor mee soup version (§4).

## 7. Beef Noodles (牛肉麵)

This is not one dish but **four unrelated traditions** that share a name in English. Keeping them apart is the point of the entry.

### 7a. Chinese Muslim / Lanzhou lamian (兰州牛肉拉面)
**Origin:** Lanzhou, Gansu; codified **1915** by **Ma Baozi (马保子)**, a **Hui Muslim** cook who hawked beef noodles from a shoulder pole before opening a shop [S26][S27]. His formulation is the *yi qing, er bai, san hong, si lü, wu huang* standard — **clear broth, white radish, red chilli oil, green herbs (coriander/scallion), yellow noodles** [S26][S27]. Hand-pulled (拉面 *lamian*) wheat noodle, halal by construction. Confidence: **high** — this is the best-documented origin claim in this entire document; Lanzhou beef noodles have a museum and a municipal standard.
**Penang relevance:** essentially nil historically; present since the 2010s only as mainland-Chinese chain restaurants.

### 7b. Taiwanese braised beef noodle (紅燒牛肉麵)
**Origin:** created **in Taiwan**, not China, by **Kuomintang veterans from Sichuan** settled in *juancun* (military dependants' villages) after 1949, first in the **Gangshan area of Kaohsiung**, using Gangshan's spicy broad-bean paste (*doubanjiang*) to make a soy-braised beef; then spreading to Taipei's Wanhua/Ximending veteran communities [S28]. Spice was toned down for a broader market. Now Taiwan's de facto national dish [S28]. Confidence: **high**.
**Penang relevance:** present as a restaurant category (Taiwanese beef-noodle shops), not a hawker tradition.

### 7c. Hainanese / Malaysian-Chinese beef noodle
This is the tradition that actually matters in Malaysia. Two sub-strands, and the sources conflict on which is primary — hence **disputed**:
- **Hainanese strand:** Hainanese migrants brought a beef-noodle tradition to Malaya; **KBN King's Seremban Beef Noodle** traces to **Goh Hian Hai**, who came from Hainan in the **1940s** and sold at the old Seremban market [S31]. **Yean Kee** in **Kluang, Johor** began as a pushcart, "Tian Le Yuan", in the **old Kluang market in 1930**, founded by **Goh Hin**, and is now run by the founder's grandson [S32]. Kheng Fatt Hainanese Beef Noodles in Singapore is the same family of dish [S32].
- **Hakka strand:** **Soong Kee Beef Noodles** (颂记牛肉丸粉) in KL was started by **Hakka** hawker-chef **Siew Koy Soong** in **1945**, immediately post-Occupation, and its house tradition is that the dish derives from **Tai Po (大埔 / Dabu) Hakka noodles** — not from Hainanese cooking at all [S29][S30]. Soong Kee's signature is dry noodles with minced beef and beef balls, soup on the side.
- One Malaysian source additionally traces "Hainanese beef noodles" to **Swatow (Shantou), Guangdong** rather than Hainan [S32] — a third attribution.

Confidence: **disputed**. What is defensible: Malaysian beef noodles are a **1930s–1940s Malayan hawker development**, made by Hainanese *and* Hakka hawkers, in the *clear/braised* idiom rather than the Lanzhou or Taiwanese ones. The convention that beef-noodle stalls are "Hainanese" is a generalisation from a few famous stalls. **Flag:** the ubiquitous blog line "Hainanese beef noodles came from Hainan Island" is not evidenced; Hainan's own cuisine is not notably beef-centric.

**Clear vs herbal.** The two Malaysian styles are:
- **Clear (清汤)**: beef-bone broth, beef slices, tripe, tendon, beef balls, coriander, fried garlic, fried shallot; the Hainanese-associated style.
- **Herbal / braised (药材 or 卤)**: broth built with Chinese medicinal herbs — dang gui, wolfberry, cinnamon, star anise — darker, sweeter, denser; associated with the Klang Valley and with Hakka stalls.
Confidence: **high** that both styles exist and are distinguished by Malaysians; **medium** on the dialect attributions.

### 7d. Malay/Indonesian soto daging and sup daging
The halal beef-noodle position in Malaysia is largely occupied not by Chinese beef noodles but by **soto daging** (beef soto) and **sup daging / sup tulang** — a Javanese-derived turmeric-and-spice broth with compressed rice (*nasi impit*), begedil, bean sprouts, fried shallot, Chinese celery, and sambal kecap; the noodle version is **mee soto** or **soto mi**, using thick yellow Hokkien noodle or bee hoon [S33][S34]. Confidence: **high**. The Javanese thread in Penang runs through Seberang Perai (see dossier 02).

**Who sells beef noodles, and the halal problem.** This is the structurally important point for a Penang culture graph. Chinese beef-noodle stalls are **not halal** — they cook in kitchens that also handle pork, use pork-bone stock or lard in adjacent dishes, and sit in Chinese coffeeshops. Malaysian Muslims therefore eat beef noodles as **soto/sup**, not as *ngau yuk fan*. Meanwhile many **Chinese Malaysians of Cantonese and Hakka background avoid beef entirely** for Buddhist/Taoist reasons (the taboo on eating the ox that ploughs the field), which is one reason beef noodles are a minority category in Chinese hawker centres despite being beloved where they exist. Penang's beef-noodle scene is thin relative to KL's and Seremban's. Confidence: **medium-high** — the halal boundary is documented; the beef taboo is well attested ethnographically but not quantified.

## 8. Fish Head Bee Hoon / Fish Soup Noodles

**Names.** 鱼头米粉 (fish head bee hoon), 鱼片米粉汤 (sliced fish bee hoon soup), 鱼汤 (fish soup). Hokkien *hû-thâu bí-hún*. Malay: *bihun sup ikan*.

**Noodle type.** **Bee hoon** (rice vermicelli) is standard; *mee tai bak*, yellow noodle, **yee mee** (fried e-fu cake, §11) and even brown-rice spaghetti are offered as alternatives at Singapore stalls [S35]. Some diners take the soup with rice or with no starch at all.

**Cultural origin.** **Teochew**, with a Hokkien overlap; developed in **Singapore**. Wikipedia dates fish soup bee hoon in Singapore to **at least the 1920s**, and credits **Swee Kee Fish Head Noodle House** with the "definitive version" in the **1970s** [S35]. The dish is on CNN's "40 Singapore foods we can't live without", and The Star describes it as being "to Singaporeans what char kway teow is to Penangites" — a revealing framing [S35]. Confidence: **medium-high** on the Teochew/Singapore attribution; **low** on the Swee Kee "definitive version" claim, which is a guidebook assertion.

**Signature elements.** Fish stock or boiled fish bones; **snakehead (*toman*)** most commonly, also pomfret, batang (Spanish mackerel), garoupa [S35]; sliced or deep-fried fish; tomato; salted vegetable (*kiam chai*); yam; ginger; **Chinese wine, brandy or cognac** splashed in at the end [S35]; fried shallot; chilli padi in light soy on the side.

**The evaporated milk question.** The white/creamy version is achieved two ways: long boiling of bones (genuine emulsion) or, far more commonly, **added evaporated milk** as a shortcut to the cloudy appearance [S36]. Singaporeans distinguish "milk soup" (奶汤) from clear soup on the menu; Teochew purists regard the milk version as inauthentic. Wikipedia lists milk among the ingredients without qualification [S35]. Confidence: **high** that both exist; **high** that the milk version is a later shortcut, though the date it emerged is undocumented. **Flag:** the frequent blog claim that evaporated milk was "a Hainanese cook's British-influenced innovation" is unsourced.

**Singapore vs Penang.** Fish soup bee hoon is a **Singapore dish, weakly represented in Penang**. Penang's Teochew hawkers again occupy an adjacent niche — koay teow th'ng with fish balls and eel-meat fish balls — and Penang's fish-noodle energy goes into **asam laksa**, a Malay/Nyonya fish-noodle in a completely different idiom (dossier 02). Penang does have Teochew fish-ball noodle and fish-porridge stalls; the specific Singapore "fish head bee hoon" format with fried fish, tomato and milk is mostly absent. Confidence: **medium-high**.

**Relationships.** Parent: Chaoshan fish-and-rice-noodle soups. Sibling: Teochew *yu wan mee* (fish ball noodle). Penang analogue by function: koay teow th'ng; Penang analogue by *ingredient* (fish broth + rice noodle): **asam laksa**, though the two share nothing else. Descendant: modern "XO fish soup" with brandy and milk.

## 9. Chee Cheong Fun (豬腸粉)

**Names.** 豬腸粉 / 猪肠粉, Cantonese *jyu cheung fan*, Hokkien *ti tn̂g hún*; Penang speakers use the Cantonese-derived form. In Hong Kong the plain rolls are **cheung fun** (腸粉) — literally "intestine noodle" — with filled variants *char siu cheung fun*, *ha cheung* etc. Mandarin *zhūchángfěn*. Malay: no distinct name; *chee cheong fun* is used directly.

**Etymology.** 豬腸 = "pig intestine", 粉 = "rice noodle". The dish contains **no offal**: the name is purely descriptive of the rolled sheet's resemblance to a length of intestine [S38]. The Hong Kong short form drops 豬 and is simply 腸粉 [S111]. Confidence: **high** on the shape explanation, which is universally reported and obviously correct on inspection; note this is a *folk-descriptive* name rather than a documented coinage.

**Noodle type.** A **steamed rice-flour sheet** — rice flour, water, sometimes tapioca or wheat starch for elasticity, poured thin onto cloth or a tray, steamed, and rolled. Served as rolls, or (Ipoh/Penang style) rolled and then **cut into short segments**.

**Cultural origin.** **Cantonese**, Guangdong; brought to Malaya by 19th-century Guangdong migrants and adopted across dialect lines [S37][S111]. The technique family extends to Vietnamese **bánh cuốn** and **bánh ướt**, Chaozhou pork-intestine rice rolls, and Taiwanese variants — a single steamed-rice-sheet family spread across the South China littoral and mainland Southeast Asia [S38]. Confidence: **high** for the Cantonese origin and the wider family; **low** for any specific inventor or town.

**Regional variants — the sauce is the dish.**
- **Penang:** the sheet is cut into segments and drenched in **hae ko** (蝦膏, thick black fermented prawn/shrimp paste — the same *petis udang* used in rojak and asam laksa) blended with **thnee cheo** (甜醬, a sweet dark-red sauce), then topped with sesame seeds, fried shallot, chilli paste and sometimes crushed peanuts [S37][S41][S38]. The result is sweet, faintly funky-fishy, and dark — unlike any other version. This is the clearest single example in the Penang repertoire of a **Cantonese carrier dish reseasoned with a Malay-archipelago fermented-seafood condiment**. Confidence: **high**.
- **Kuala Lumpur:** sweet fermented **bean paste** predominates, plus sesame, fried shallot, chilli [S40].
- **Ipoh:** two modes — *dry*, tossed in soy, shallot oil and sesame oil, topped with **har mai** (dried shrimp), served with bright red sweet sauce, chilli sauce and pickled green chilli; and *wet*, flooded with **curry** (with pork skin and long bean) or a minced-pork-and-shiitake gravy. The Pasir Pinji style is the reference [S39].
- **Hong Kong:** the sheet is the point, not the sauce — served plain with light soy and sesame, or filled with char siu, prawn, or beef, as a dim sum item; peanut sauce and hoisin at street stalls [S38][S111].

**Penang-specific variation, summarised.** Same noodle, radically different flavour vector: Penang is the only version whose sauce is built on **fermented shrimp** rather than soy, bean or sesame. Confidence: **high**.

**Relationships.** Parent: Cantonese *cheung fun*. Siblings: bánh cuốn / bánh ướt (Vietnam), Chaozhou rice rolls, Ipoh and KL variants. Shared-condiment relatives *within Penang*: **rojak** and **asam laksa** (both use hae ko) — a Penang-internal link that crosses the Chinese/Malay line via a single condiment. Adjacent sold-together dishes: Penang chee cheong fun carts frequently sit beside koay teow th'ng and loh bak stalls.

## 10. Koay Chiap / Kway Chap (粿汁)

**Names.** 粿汁, Pe̍h-ōe-jī **kóe-chiap**, Mandarin *guǒzhī*; spelled kway chap, kuay jap, kway jap, and in Penang commonly **koay chiap** [S42]. Teochew pronunciation *guê2 zab4*.

**Etymology.** 粿 *kóe* = rice-flour cake/sheet; 汁 *chiap* = juice, broth, gravy. "Rice-sheet in gravy" [S42]. Crucially, **汁 chiap ≠ 湯 th'ng** — kway chap is named for a *thick, soy-dark braising liquid*, not a clear soup, which distinguishes it from koay teow th'ng at the level of the name.

**The Penang naming confusion.** Penang Hokkien speakers say *koay chiap* for this dish and *koay teow* for flat rice noodles; the phonetic proximity of **koay chiap / koay teow / "koay chiak"** produces persistent confusion in English-language writing and on menus, and Penang stalls frequently sell both, sometimes from one cart. They are different dishes: koay teow is a **cut ribbon** in a **clear** broth; koay chiap is a **broad flat sheet or large irregular square**, loosely folded, in a **dark braise**. Confidence: **high** on the distinction; the "koay chiak" spelling appears in Penang menus and blogs and is best treated as an orthographic variant, not a separate dish.

**Noodle type.** **Broad, flat rice sheets** — wider and floppier than koay teow, closer to a lasagne sheet or an unrolled chee cheong fun sheet, sometimes folded into loose triangles [S42][S43].

**Cultural origin.** **Teochew**, Chaoshan. Roots.gov.sg (Singapore's National Heritage Board) treats kway chap as intangible cultural heritage brought by Teochew migrants; one popular source dates the initial form to the **Ming dynasty, ~400 years ago**, which should be read as folk-chronology rather than evidence [S43][S44]. In Singapore it was a roadside-stall dish until **1960s public-health regulation** pushed sellers into hawker centres and coffeeshops [S42]. Confidence: **high** on Teochew provenance; **low** on the Ming dating.

**Broth by geography — this is the diagnostic table.**
- **Chaoshan (home region):** broth is **white/opaque**, made with **rice milk** — the ancestral form is not soy-dark at all [S42].
- **Singapore/Malaysia:** **dark soy** braise with five-spice, star anise, cassia, garlic — the *lou* master stock [S42][S44].
- **Thailand:** **clear** broth with local spicing [S42].
This three-way split is one of the cleanest documented cases of a single diaspora dish diverging by host country. Confidence: **high** (Wikipedia, sourced to Roots and the Straits Times).

**Ingredients.** Braised pork belly, **pig intestine, pig ear, pig stomach, pig skin**, braised hard-boiled egg, firm and puffed beancurd (*tau kwa*, *tau pok*), preserved salted vegetable (*kiam chai*), peanuts; chilli-vinegar dip on the side [S42].

**Penang-specific variation.** **Penang substitutes duck for pork.** Wikipedia states it plainly: "In Penang, Malaysia, duck offal and meat make up the sides instead of pork" [S42]. Penang koay chiap stalls — the Kimberley Street stall and Restoran Kimberly are the reference points, plus Mr Poh's stall — serve duck meat, duck offal, pig's ear, braised egg and beancurd, and are overwhelmingly **evening/supper** trade [S45][S46]. This is consistent with the Penang Teochew duck specialisation noted in §6: Penang's Teochew hawkers built their whole repertoire around duck. Confidence: **high**.

**Relationships.** Sibling: duck koay teow th'ng (§6) — same braise, different starch, different broth clarity. Parent: Chaoshan rice-milk kway chap. Cousin: Singapore *lor mee* (also a soy-dark starch-thickened noodle, but Hokkien not Teochew, §18). Technique parent: the Teochew *lou* master stock, shared with lou ark and *lor bak*.

## 11. Yi Mein / E-Fu Noodles (伊麵 / 伊府麵)

**Names.** 伊麵 / 伊府麵; Cantonese **yi min / yee fu min**; Mandarin *yī miàn / yīfǔ miàn*; Hokkien-influenced Malaysian spelling **yee mee**; Indonesian **i fu mie**; English e-fu / ee-fu noodles [S47]. Also 壽麵 *sau mein* ("longevity noodle") and 長壽麵 when served at birthdays [S47].

**Etymology.** "The **Yi family's** noodle" — named for Qing official **Yi Bingshou** (伊秉綬, 1754–1815), calligrapher, poet, and prefect of Huizhou, Guangdong; 府 *fu* denotes the official's residence/prefecture [S47][S48]. A secondary legend has the poet **Song Xiang** tasting the dish and remarking that so fine a delicacy deserved a proper name, whereupon it was called *yi fu mein* [S48].

**Origin legend.** Two competing tellings, both traditional and neither documented contemporaneously:
1. **Deliberate invention:** Yi instructed his cook to make a wheat-and-egg dough, sun-dried, deep-fried golden, and stored — a *shelf-stable pre-cooked noodle* to be rehydrated when guests arrived unexpectedly [S48][S51].
2. **Happy accident:** a cook at one of Yi's banquets dropped noodles into hot oil instead of broth, drained them, and served them under a sauce [S48].
The noodle is often further traced to **Ninghua, Fujian**, Yi's ancestral county, before its Huizhou/Cantonese career [S48]. Confidence: **medium** for the Yi Bingshou attribution as *traditional* (it is consistent across Chinese and Western sources and Wikipedia records it as the traditional credit [S47]); **low** that either specific anecdote is historical. **Flag:** the widely-repeated claim that yi mein is "the precursor of instant noodles" is a rhetorical framing, not a demonstrated line of descent to Ando Momofuku's 1958 product — though the technical parallel (fry-dry-then-rehydrate) is real and belongs in the graph as a *technique* edge rather than an *ancestry* edge [S49].

**Noodle type.** Flat **wheat-and-egg noodle made with lye water**, boiled or steamed, then **deep-fried and dried into a flat brick or patty**; rehydrated by boiling before final cooking [S47]. Springy, spongy, faintly chewy, golden-brown. Sold dried in every Malaysian supermarket.

**Cultural origin.** **Cantonese** in its developed form (Guangdong/Hong Kong banquet cooking), Fujian-adjacent in its legendary origin [S47][S48]. Reached Malaysia with Cantonese migration and became ubiquitous across dialect lines because the dried brick is cheap, keeps, and cooks in two minutes.

**Signature preparations.** Canonical banquet form: **乾燒伊麵** *gon siu yi min*, braised dry with **shiitake mushroom and yellow Chinese chives (韮黃 *gau wong*)** in oyster sauce — the two are near-obligatory [S47][S50]. Also crab-meat yi mein (蟹肉伊麵), lobster yi mein (龍蝦伊麵, sometimes with cheese in Hong Kong), and in soup [S47].

**Symbolism.** Served at **birthdays** as 長壽麵 / 壽麵, and at **Lunar New Year**; the strands must **not be cut**, and each strand should be eaten whole [S47]. Yi mein is specifically noted as the main dish at **Hakka birthday banquets** [S48]. See §19.

**Malaysian/Penang usage.** In Malaysia "**yee mee**" is everyday coffeeshop food, not banquet food: the dried brick is used for *claypot yee mee*, *wat tan yee mee* (egg-gravy yee mee, §21), and soupy yee mee with fishcake and liver — a cheap, fast, soft-noodle option [S48]. Penang stalls sell it as an interchangeable noodle option alongside koay teow, bee hoon and yellow mee. Confidence: **high**.

**Relationships.** Technique cousin: **Tuaran mee** (§3), which independently uses fry-then-boil. Preparation sibling: **wat tan hor** (§21) — the same egg gravy is poured over either hor fun or yee mee. Symbolic sibling: **mee sua** (§19), the other longevity noodle. Descendant category: industrial instant noodles (technique only, not lineage).

## 12. Ipoh Hor Fun (河粉) / Kai Si Hor Fun (雞絲河粉)

**Names.** 河粉 *hor fun* (Cantonese) = 沙河粉 *sa hor fun*, "Shahe noodle"; **kai si hor fun** 雞絲河粉 = "shredded chicken hor fun"; Ipoh version 怡保雞絲河粉 [S52][S53]. Same physical noodle as Hokkien/Teochew **koay teow** — the difference is dialect vocabulary, not product. Malay: *kuetiau*.

**Etymology.** 沙河 *Shahe* is a district of **Guangzhou** (in Tianhe District) after which the flat rice noodle is named; 河粉 is the clipped form [S53][S52]. *Kai si* = chicken shreds.

**Noodle type.** **Flat rice noodle**, notably *thinner and silkier* in Ipoh than the standard koay teow, cut narrow.

**Cultural origin.** **Cantonese**, Guangzhou, via the Kinta Valley tin rush. Ipoh Echo's account: early-20th-century Guangdong and Hakka migrants to "Paloh" (Ipoh) brought the **Tianhe District speciality** with them and began selling it to fellow miners [S52]. Confidence: **high** for the Cantonese-Guangzhou origin of the noodle; **medium** for the specific Tianhe transmission story.

**The interesting complication.** Ipoh Echo reports that the **two most celebrated kai si hor fun stalls — Thean Chun and the stall in Loke Wooi Kee coffeeshop — were founded by Hokkien immigrants from Nan'an, Fujian**, not by Cantonese [S52]. The article's own reading: the Hokkien palate is accustomed to combining **meat and seafood** in one bowl (as in Hokkien mee), which is why the Ipoh version is a **prawn-and-chicken** broth rather than a pure chicken one. The prawn element — heads and shells from **Tanjung Tualang** freshwater prawns, boiled down to give the broth its orange tint and depth — is the **local innovation not present in the Chinese original** [S52]. Confidence: **medium-high**; this is a single well-informed local-newspaper account, but it is specific, internally coherent, and names stalls and a source county, which is better than most.

**Signature elements.** Silky thin hor fun; clear broth of chicken **plus prawn heads/shells**; poached shredded chicken; blanched prawns; chives or spring onion; served with **pickled green chilli** in soy. Frequently accompanied by Ipoh's other specialities — *nga choy kai* (bean sprout chicken), *sar hor fun*.

**"Sar hor fun".** *Sar hor fun* (沙河粉) is simply the full name of the noodle; in Malaysian usage "sar hor fun" often denotes the **soup version** of Ipoh hor fun specifically, as against *wat tan hor* (the fried, egg-gravy version, §21). Confidence: **medium** — usage varies by state and the terms are not policed.

**The limestone-water claim — evaluated.** The claim that Ipoh's noodles, bean sprouts, white coffee and *tau fu fah* owe their quality to **mineral-rich water from the surrounding karst limestone hills** is universally repeated in food writing and tourism material [S53]. Assessment:
- The **geology is real**: Ipoh sits in the Kinta Valley, ringed by Paleozoic limestone karst, and the groundwater is genuinely hard (high dissolved calcium and magnesium).
- The **bean sprout claim** is the strongest of the family: Ipoh *nga choy* are visibly and reproducibly plumper and crunchier than elsewhere, and hard water plausibly affects sprout cell-wall development. Even here the mechanism is asserted, not demonstrated.
- The **hor fun claim** is **not evidenced**. No published analysis links Ipoh water chemistry to rice-noodle texture. Rice-noodle texture is dominated by rice variety, flour age, starch ratio, hydration and steaming technique — variables entirely under the noodle-maker's control. A hardness effect is not implausible (calcium ions do interact with starch gelatinisation) but nobody has shown it.
Confidence: **low / unevidenced**. **Flag this loudly** — it is the single most-repeated unexamined claim in Malaysian food writing. The honest formulation is: *Ipoh has a concentrated, competitive, multi-generational noodle-making trade, and attributes its results to the water.*

**Relationships.** Parent: Guangzhou Shahe fen. Siblings: **wat tan hor** (§21, fried with egg gravy), Penang **koay teow th'ng** (§6, same noodle in a Teochew clear broth), **char koay teow** (same noodle, Hokkien/Teochew fried). Local partner dish: nga choy kai.

## 13. Bee Hoon / Mee Hoon (米粉)

**Names.** 米粉, Hokkien **bí-hún** (whence Malay **bihun**, Indonesian *bihun*), Mandarin *mǐfěn*, Cantonese *mai fan*; Teochew *bí-hun*. English: rice vermicelli. Malay **bihun**; Malaysian Tamil speakers also use *bihun*. Note that **the international name for the product is a Hokkien word** — "bee hoon" and "bihun" both descend from 米粉 read in Southern Min, which is a direct index of who carried the product around the world [S54].

**Etymology.** 米 rice + 粉 flour/noodle. Undisputed.

**Noodle type.** Extruded **rice-flour** thread, dried; thin (0.5–1 mm), brittle when dry, soaked or blanched before use. Distinct from **tang hoon / glass noodle** (冬粉 / 粉絲, mung-bean starch, translucent when cooked) — a distinction Malaysian menus routinely blur in English.

**Cultural origin.** **Fujian**, specifically the Hokkien coastal belt. Two production centres matter:
- **Xiamen / southern Fujian** — *chao mifen* (炒米粉), stir-fried rice vermicelli, is the archetypal Hokkien dish and travelled with Hokkien migration to Taiwan, Singapore, Malaysia, Indonesia and the Philippines (*pancit bihon*) [S54][S57].
- **Putian (莆田), i.e. Heng Hwa / Xinghua (興化)** — a coastal city between Fuzhou and Xiamen whose bee hoon is a recognised speciality product, **finer and silkier** than standard, and whose diaspora dish **fried Heng Hwa bee hoon** is a distinct named item in Singapore and Malaysia [S55][S56]. The Heng Hwa are a small, distinct dialect group (Puxian Min, neither Hokkien nor Foochow) and their bee hoon is their culinary calling card.
Confidence: **high**.

**Fried vs soup — the two idioms.**
- **Char bee hoon** (炒米粉): the Hokkien home/hawker staple. Rice vermicelli wok-fried with garlic, shallot, egg, cabbage or choy sum, bean sprouts, dried shrimp, sometimes pork or fishcake; seasoned with light and dark soy and white pepper. In Penang this is **char mee hoon** ("char" = fried in Hokkien) and is the backbone of the **"economy bee hoon"** breakfast format — a tray of fried bee hoon plus fried noodles plus assorted fried side items, sold by selection, ubiquitous in Penang coffeeshops. Confidence: **high**.
- **Soup**: bee hoon as the neutral carrier in fish soup (§8), sliced-fish soup, prawn mee, curry mee, lor mee, and the Penang habit of mixing bee hoon with yellow mee in one bowl — a Penang signature so routine it is rarely commented on. Confidence: **high**.

**The "Singapore noodles" fiction.** *Singapore-style noodles* / 星洲炒米 is a **Hong Kong invention**, not a Singaporean dish. The consensus account: Cantonese chefs in **post-WWII Hong Kong** (variously dated late 1940s–1960s) stir-fried rice vermicelli with **curry powder** — abundant in the colony via British-Indian trade — plus char siu, prawn, egg, onion, capsicum and bean sprouts, and named it after Singapore for exotic appeal [S58][S59][S60]. Singaporean chef Damian D'Silva puts the invention in Hong Kong in the 1950s–60s [S59]. The dish **is not sold in Singapore** except as a novelty for tourists; Singapore's own *fried bee hoon* uses no curry powder at all [S58][S60]. Confidence: **high** that it is not Singaporean; **medium** on the specific decade and mechanism. This is a useful node in a culture graph precisely because it is a *fictitious geographic attribution* — a category the graph should be able to represent.

**Penang relevance.** Bee hoon is the second-most-used noodle in Penang after yellow mee, and appears in curry mee, prawn mee, char bee hoon, economy bee hoon, and mee udang; asam laksa uses its thick round rice-noodle cousin. Penang's bee hoon culture is Hokkien-standard rather than distinctive.

**Relationships.** Parent: Fujian *mifen*. Regional descendants: Taiwanese *chao mifen*, Filipino *pancit bihon*, Indonesian *bihun goreng*, Thai *sen mee*. Distinct products often confused with it: **tang hoon** (mung-bean glass noodle) and **kuay teow** (rice sheet — same grain, different form). Fictional relative: "Singapore noodles".

## 14. Bihun Goreng / Mee Hoon Goreng

**Names.** Malay **bihun goreng** ("fried rice vermicelli"), **mi hun goreng**; Chinese equivalent **char bee hoon** 炒米粉. Mamak-stall version marketed as *bihun goreng mamak*. A Malaysian mamak menu item confusingly labelled *bihun goreng Singapore* also exists, referencing the Hong Kong curry-powder dish (§13) [S62].

**Etymology.** *Goreng* = "fried" in Malay. The name is Malay; the noodle name is Hokkien; the dish is therefore lexically hybrid on its face.

**Noodle type.** Rice vermicelli, identical product to §13.

**The distinction that matters — three parallel traditions using the same noodle:**
1. **Chinese char bee hoon** (Hokkien): soy-forward, garlic and shallot base, dried shrimp, egg, cabbage, bean sprouts, white pepper, lard or oil; savoury and comparatively pale [S62].
2. **Malay bihun goreng**: built on a **dried-chilli sambal / rempah** base — pounded dried chilli, shallot, garlic, sometimes belacan — with kicap manis, tomato, egg, bean sprout, tofu puffs, and often prawn or chicken; fragrant, lightly spicy, no pork [S62].
3. **Mamak (Indian-Muslim) bihun goreng**: chilli paste plus **tomato ketchup** and chilli sauce, sweeter and tangier than Malay style and milder than mee goreng mamak; egg, tofu, potato, bean sprout, cabbage, sometimes mutton or *sotong*; finished with a squeeze of lime [S61][S63]. The mamak style is the direct sibling of **mee goreng mamak**, the yellow-noodle version and the more famous dish — Wikipedia describes mee goreng mamak explicitly as a fusion using **Chinese yellow noodles with Malay and Indian seasoning**, sold by Indian-Muslim hawkers [S61].

Confidence: **high** that the three traditions are distinct and locally recognised; **medium** on any specific ingredient list, which varies by stall.

**Penang-specific.** Penang is one of the mamak heartlands (dossier 02) and its mamak *goreng* dishes are unusually developed — including **mee sotong** (§22a), a Penang-only mamak fried noodle. The Penang Chinese char bee hoon expression is the **economy bee hoon** breakfast tray. The two coexist street-by-street without merging, because of the halal boundary. Confidence: **high**.

**Relationships.** Sibling: mee goreng mamak (same seasoning, yellow noodle). Sibling: kuetiau goreng, nasi goreng (same seasoning logic, different starch). Parent (noodle): Fujian bee hoon. Parent (seasoning): Malay/Indian-Muslim rempah-and-chilli-paste cooking. Adjacent: Indonesian *bihun goreng*, closer to the Malay style with heavier kecap manis.

## 15. "You Mee" / "Yu Mee" — disambiguation of a genuinely ambiguous term

The brief flags ambiguity here and is right to. There are **at least four distinct referents** in Malaysian usage, and the confusion is compounded by inconsistent romanisation. Ranked by how often each is meant:

**(a) 幼麵 — *youmian* / Cantonese *yau min* — "fine noodle". THE MOST COMMON REFERENT.**
幼 in Cantonese means *fine, thin, delicate* (not "young", which is the Mandarin sense that misleads). *Yau min* is the thin round wheat-and-egg noodle used for wonton noodles across Hong Kong, Guangdong, Malaysia and Singapore, often made with whole egg (*cyun daan min* 全蛋麵) [S21]. Wikipedia's Mee pok article explicitly equates **mee kia** with "wonton-style noodles or **youmian**" [S18] — so *you mee* (Cantonese) and *mee kia* (Hokkien/Teochew) are the same noodle under two dialect names.
**Critically, in Malaysian pan mee shops "you mee" is a menu option, not a dish.** Wikipedia's Banmian article lists it as one of the handmade-noodle forms alongside ban mian and mee hoon kueh: *"youmian (similar dough texture and taste, but thinner round noodles)"* [S1]. When a Klang Valley pan mee stall's board reads 板面 / 面粉粿 / 幼面, it is offering thick-cut, hand-torn, and thin-round versions of the **same dough**. Confidence: **high**. This is the answer to the brief's question in the pan-mee context.

**(b) 伊麵 — "yee mee" — e-fu noodle.** The deep-fried dried egg-noodle brick (§11), spelled *yee mee* in Malaysia and pronounced close enough to "yu mee" to collide [S47][S48]. When a Malaysian coffeeshop menu says "yee mee", it means this. Confidence: **high**.

**(c) 魚麵 — "yu mee" — actual fish noodle.** A real product: noodles made from **pounded fish paste**, sometimes with no wheat flour at all. Associated with **Foochow** communities — Sarawak (Sibu, Serikei), **Sitiawan** in Perak, Yong Peng in Johor — and with Sabah's fresh-fish-noodle stalls [S101][S102]. A genuine, distinct, low-frequency item. Confidence: **medium-high**.

**(d) "Oil-tossed dry noodle".** The brief's hypothesis — that *you mee* means "oil noodle" (油麵, *yau min* in Cantonese / *iû-mī* in Hokkien) — is **also a real term but refers to something else again**: 油麵 is the standard Chinese name for **fresh yellow alkaline wheat noodle** (the "oil noodle" of Malaysian and Taiwanese usage, oiled to prevent sticking). In Malaysia this is almost always just called *mee* or *mee kuning*. Confidence: **medium**.

**Practical rule for the graph.** Treat "you mee / yau min 幼麵" as a **noodle-type** node (thin round egg noodle ≡ mee kia ≡ wonton noodle), "yee mee 伊麵" as a **distinct noodle-type** node (fried dried egg brick), "yu mee 魚麵" as a **rare regional noodle-type** node (Foochow fish-paste noodle), and 油麵 as an **alias of yellow alkaline noodle**. Do not merge them. Flag: nearly every English-language food blog that uses the phrase "you mee" does so without specifying which of these it means.

## 16. Thai Boat Noodles (ก๋วยเตี๋ยวเรือ) and Tom Yum Noodles

### 16a. Boat noodles — kuay teow reua
**Names.** ก๋วยเตี๋ยวเรือ, RTGS *kuaitiao ruea*, "kway teow boat" [S64]. Note again: **ก๋วยเตี๋ยว *kuaitiao* is a loan of Teochew 粿條 *kóe-tiâu*** — the Thai word for rice noodle is Teochew, as *bamee* is Teochew for egg noodle. Thai noodle culture is structurally Chinese-derived at the level of vocabulary.

**Noodle type.** Diner's choice across the standard Thai range: *sen yai* (wide rice), *sen lek* (narrow rice), *sen mee* (vermicelli), *bamee* (egg) [S64].

**Origin.** Central Thailand's canal country. Wikipedia locates the tradition in **Ayutthaya and Rangsit** (Pathum Thani province), with **Victory Monument, Bangkok** now the best-known cluster [S64]. The standard account: Chinese (largely Teochew) settlers along the Rangsit canals sold noodles from boats; the **small bowl** exists because a single vendor paddled, cooked, served, took money and washed up alone, and a large bowl would spill in the hand-off from boat to bank [S64][S65][S66]. Confidence: **high** on the boat-vending mechanism and the small-bowl explanation (functional and internally consistent); **medium** on Rangsit as the specific origin.

**Flag:** several sources assert boat noodles were sold from canoes in **Ayutthaya between the 12th and 16th centuries** [S66]. This is almost certainly **anachronistic**: the dish's own name is a Teochew loanword, and mass Teochew settlement in Siam dates from the late 18th century onward. Canal-boat food vending in Ayutthaya is plausible; *kuay teow reua as we know it* is a late-19th/20th-century dish, and Rangsit's canals themselves were dug in the **1890s** under the Rangsit irrigation project. Confidence in the medieval claim: **low / likely false**.

**Signature elements.** Both **pork and beef** in the same bowl; **dark soy**; **fermented bean curd** (*tao hu yi*); cinnamon, star anise; meatballs; pig's liver; morning glory (*pak bung*); bean sprouts; fried garlic; pork cracklings; holy basil; Thai chilli flakes — and the defining thickener, **fresh pig's or cow's blood** (เลือดหมูสด / เลือดเนื้อสด) mixed with salt and spices, which gives the broth its body and near-black colour [S64]. The commonly-repeated claim that the dark broth existed to *hide* blood residue from butchering is a food-media rationalisation, not documented [S65]. Confidence: **high** on the blood as ingredient; **low** on the concealment story.

**How they reach Penang.** Boat noodles are a **21st-century import to Malaysia**, arriving through (i) Thai restaurant chains in the Klang Valley and Penang malls, (ii) the northern-Malaysian appetite for Thai food generally, given Penang's Kedah/Siamese hinterland (dossier 02), and (iii) cross-border traffic through Bukit Kayu Hitam and Padang Besar. They are a *restaurant* dish in Penang, not a hawker-cart dish, and the blood component is often omitted for local palates. Confidence: **medium** — well observed, poorly documented.

### 16b. Tom yum noodles
**Names.** *kuay teow tom yum* (ก๋วยเตี๋ยวต้มยำ); Malaysian spelling **tomyam mee / tomyam bihun**. Malay: *mi tom yam*.

**What it is in Thailand:** a **dry-ish or lightly-souped** noodle bowl dressed with the tom-yum flavour set — lime, fish sauce, chilli, sugar, ground peanut, sometimes chilli jam and evaporated milk (*tom yum nam khon*) — not necessarily a full tom yum soup.

**What it is in Penang:** effectively a **naturalised local dish**. Penang tom yam is sold in Chinese coffeeshops, at Malay warung, and at Thai-run stalls; **Raja Uda and Butterworth** on the mainland are a recognised cluster, and Gurney Drive on the island [S91]. The Penang style is generally a **thick, red, chilli-paste-heavy** broth with a heavy hand of lime and *bunga kantan*, distinguished by local writers from the **clear, tangy, herb-forward** Thai-style broth [S91]. Customers select their own ingredients from a tray — vegetables, fish cake, fish ball, prawn, squid — which is a Malaysian hawker convention, not a Thai one [S91]. A Michelin Bib entry, **Ghee Lian**, opened on Perak Road in 2012 with a **green tom yum** noodle among three noodle dishes — local invention within an imported category [S92]. Confidence: **medium-high**.

**Relationships.** Boat noodles: parent = Chaoshan kway teow soup + central-Thai canal vending; sibling = Thai *kuay teow nuea pueai* (beef noodle). Tom yum noodles: parent = Thai tom yum + Chinese noodle bowl; Penang siblings = **curry mee** and **asam laksa**, in the sense that all three are sour-hot noodle soups occupying the same slot in the Penang eater's week; northern-peninsula cousin = the whole southern-Thai/Kedah sour-fish idiom (dossier 02).

## 17. Hokkien Char Mee / KL-style Hokkien Mee (福建炒麵)

**Names.** KL: **Hokkien mee**, **Hokkien char mee** (福建炒麵, "Hokkien fried noodle"), locally also *tai lok mee* (大碌麵) after the noodle used [S67][S95]. Penang: **Hokkien char** (福建炒), to distinguish it from Penang's own **Hokkien mee**, which is a *prawn soup* noodle [S93][S94].

**Etymology.** "Hokkien noodle" — named after the maker's dialect group, not after any place or ingredient. This is the entire source of the naming disaster (see §18).

**Noodle type.** KL: **thick yellow alkaline wheat noodle**, notably fat — *tai lok mee*, sometimes 4–5 mm in diameter [S95]. Penang Hokkien char: standard yellow mee, usually **mixed with bee hoon** [S93][S94].

**Origin story — KL.** Attributed to **Ong Kim Lian**, a migrant from **Anxi county, Fujian**, who arrived in Malaya around **1905**, could not find work, and began hawking noodles. He is said to have started with a starchy Hokkien noodle soup of the kind eaten at celebrations, then, as fried-noodle competition intensified, developed a distinctive style: **thick yellow noodles, heavy dark soy, pork lard, braise-frying in a broth of pork bones, prawn heads and *ti poh* (dried flounder)**, finished with crisp lard croutons and high wok heat. In **1927** he moved to Petaling Street, Chinatown, from Dang Wangi and founded **Kim Lian Kee** (金蓮記), which still operates [S67][S68][S69]. Asked what the dish was called, he reportedly said "Hokkien mee", because he was Hokkien [S67].

Confidence: **medium**. The 1927 Kim Lian Kee founding date is consistent across independent sources and the business has a continuous 90+ year history on one street, which is real evidence [S68][S69]. The *invention* claim is family-sourced and unverifiable, and note a source inconsistency: at least one retelling refers to the founder as "Wong" mid-article while the name given elsewhere is Ong [S67] — a sign the narrative has been copied between outlets without checking. **Flag:** treat "Ong Kim Lian invented KL Hokkien mee in 1927" as a strong tradition, not a fact.

**Signature elements (KL).** Thick yellow noodle; **dark soy** (a lot of it); **pork lard** and **chee yau char** (crisp lard croutons) as garnish, non-negotiable; pork slices, prawns, squid, fishcake, cabbage or choy sum; the noodles are *braise-fried* — fried then simmered in stock until the sauce clings — rather than dry stir-fried; served with **sambal belacan** on the side [S67][S95].

**Signature elements (Penang Hokkien char).** Yellow mee **plus bee hoon**; only a *tinge* of dark soy, producing a light brown rather than black dish; prawns, pork, egg, bean sprout, chives; smoky *wok hei* dominant over sauce [S93][S94]. Penang Hokkien char is closer in spirit to char koay teow than to KL Hokkien mee. Confidence: **high** — this contrast is reported consistently.

**Why it shares a name with Penang prawn mee.** Because *both* were named by their makers after the makers' dialect group, in different cities, at roughly the same period, with no coordination. In **Penang**, Hokkien hawkers' signature was a **prawn-head-and-pork-bone soup noodle**, so "Hokkien mee" came to mean that. In **KL**, a Hokkien hawker's signature was a **dark fried noodle**, so "Hokkien mee" came to mean that. In **Singapore**, Hokkien hawkers around Rochor sold a **wet fried prawn-and-squid noodle with bee hoon and yellow mee**, so "Hokkien mee" means that there [S70][S71][S95]. Three dishes, one name, one ethnonym, zero relationship beyond the makers' shared ancestry. Confidence: **high** — this is the single most important naming fact in Malaysian noodle culture and it is well documented.

Penangites resolve the collision by calling the local soup dish **Hokkien mee** (or *hae mee*, 蝦麵, "prawn noodle") and the fried one **Hokkien char**; KL people call the soup version **prawn mee** and the fried one **Hokkien mee** [S71][S93][S94].

**Relationships.** Same-name-different-dish: Penang Hokkien mee (prawn soup); Singapore Hokkien mee (Rochor-style wet fry). Technique sibling: **lor mee** (§18) — also Hokkien, also starchy/braised. Descendant: the "Hokkien mee" served across Malaysian food courts, generally the KL style.

## 18. Loh Mee vs Lam Mee vs Hokkien Mee — disambiguation

This section exists because the terms are systematically confused, including by Malaysian sources.

**Lor mee / loh mee (滷麵 / 卤面).** *Lor* = 滷, "to braise in a master stock"; *mee* = noodle. A **Hokkien** dish, traced to **Zhangzhou, Fujian** [S72][S73]. Thick yellow noodle in a **starch-thickened dark gravy** made with cornflour, five-spice, dark soy and beaten egg; topped with braised pork, fried battered fish, braised egg, *ngoh hiang*; finished at the table with **minced garlic, chilli, and Chinese black vinegar** [S72]. The black vinegar is essential and diagnostic. Confidence: **high**.

**Penang lor mee** is a recognised local sub-variant, described by Malaysian food writers as **thicker and more intensely flavoured** than the Singapore or southern-Malaysian versions [S103]. There is also **Ulu Yam loh mee** (Selangor), a distinct dish: a *vinegar-forward braised noodle soup*, less starchy, from the Ulu Yam area.

**Lam mee.** In **Penang**, lam mee is **Nyonya birthday noodles**: yellow noodle in a **clear, long-simmered pork-rib or chicken-carcass broth finished with prawns**, topped with shredded chicken, prawn, pork belly, dried scallop, shredded omelette and bean sprouts, and served with **sambal belacan**, without which Penangites say it is not lam mee [S74][S75][S76][S77]. It is a **home and celebration dish** more than a hawker dish, though hawker versions exist (Ah Lim's on Jalan Macalister is a known one) [S75]. Confidence: **high** on the description; the dish is well documented in Penang Nyonya cooking.

**The etymological problem.** One widely-circulated account (foodpanda Malaysia) claims lam mee *is* loh mee: that loh mee entered northern Malaysia in the 1950s, spread south to Ipoh and KL, was lightened for Cantonese palates, and had its name re-pronounced in Cantonese as "lam mee" [S74]. **Assess as low confidence / probably wrong.** Problems: (i) Penang lam mee has a *clear* broth and loh mee a *thick starchy* one — they are not the same dish lightened, they are different dishes; (ii) Penang lam mee is a Nyonya *birthday* dish with a ritual function loh mee does not have; (iii) the phonetic story runs the wrong way geographically (Penang is not Cantonese-majority); (iv) 淋 *lam* ("to pour over") and 濫/*lām* ("to mix") in Hokkien are perfectly good independent etymologies for a noodle over which broth is poured. **Flag as a food-media claim without evidence.**

**The map:**

| Term | Dialect group | Broth | Diagnostic | Where |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Lor mee** 滷麵 | Hokkien (Zhangzhou) | thick, starch-bound, dark | black vinegar + garlic at table | Singapore, Penang, Johor, Klang Valley |
| **Lam mee** | Hokkien / Nyonya | clear, prawn-sweetened | sambal belacan; birthday context | Penang and Nyonya households |
| **Hokkien mee (Penang)** | Hokkien | clear-red prawn-and-pork broth | prawn heads, sambal, hard-boiled egg | Penang |
| **Hokkien mee (KL)** | Hokkien | none — braise-fried | dark soy + lard croutons, thick noodle | Klang Valley |
| **Hokkien mee (Singapore)** | Hokkien | wet fry with prawn stock | mixed yellow mee + bee hoon, lime, sambal | Singapore |

Confidence on the table: **high**, except the lam mee etymology (**low**).

**Rule for the graph.** Model "Hokkien mee" as a **name node with three disjoint dish referents** disambiguated by city, and "lor mee"/"lam mee" as **separate dishes frequently conflated in secondary sources**. Record the conflation as an edge type — *confused-with* — distinct from *derived-from*.

## 19. Longevity Noodles / Birthday Noodles / CNY Noodles (長壽麵)

**Names.** 長壽麵 *cháng shòu miàn* (longevity noodle), 壽麵 *shòu miàn*; **mee sua** 麵線 (Hokkien *mī-sòaⁿ*, "noodle thread"), also *misua*, *mee suah*; 生日麵 (birthday noodle). Penang Nyonya: **lam mee** (§18). Cantonese banquet form: **yi mein** as *sau mein* (§11).

**Noodle type — and this is dialect-specific:**
- **Hokkien / Teochew / Foochow households: mee sua** (麵線), an extremely fine salted wheat thread, hand-pulled and sun-dried, cooking in under a minute [S78][S79]. Sarawak and **Sitiawan** (Perak) are the Malaysian hand-pulled mee sua centres, both **Foochow** strongholds [S101].
- **Cantonese and Hakka banquets: yi mein** (伊麵), served *gon siu* with chives and mushroom [S47][S48].
- **Northern-Chinese-influenced settings:** long hand-pulled wheat noodle.
Confidence: **high**.

**The customs.**
- **Birthday.** Hokkien Malaysians and Singaporeans serve mee sua in a **sesame-oil-and-rice-wine broth** (or clear chicken broth) with a **hard-boiled egg dyed red**. Long unbroken strands = long life; **the noodles must not be cut**, and ideally each strand is eaten whole; the red egg = luck and prosperity, extending the *full-month* red-egg custom for newborns to all birthdays [S78][S79][S80]. Confidence: **high**.
- **Foochow variant:** *ang jiu mee sua* — mee sua in **red glutinous-rice-wine lees (红糟) chicken soup**, the Foochow signature, standard for birthdays, post-partum confinement and Chinese New Year in Sitiawan and Sarawak [S101]. Confidence: **high**.
- **Chinese New Year.** Yi mein and long noodles are eaten for the same reason; the no-cutting rule is explicit in the tradition [S47][S80]. Yi mein is specifically named as the main dish at **Hakka birthday banquets** [S48].
- **Nyonya birthday:** **lam mee** with sambal belacan, in Penang (§18) [S76].
- **Chives** are added because Hokkiens associate 韭菜 *gau chai* (whose 韭 is homophonous with 久 *gau*, "long time") with longevity [S78].

**Yee sang adjacency.** *Yee sang / lou sang* (魚生 / 撈生) is not a noodle dish but occupies the adjacent slot on the Chinese Malaysian CNY table and shares the *auspicious-eating* grammar (the higher the toss, the better the year). **Origin: disputed, leaning Malaysian.** The Malaysian claim: created in the **1940s at Loke Ching Kee restaurant, Seremban**, by **Loke Ching Fatt**, as "Tenth Sense Raw Fish" (十感鱼生), a thirty-ingredient plum-sauce dish tossed on the pavement with shouted auspicious phrases [S81][S82]. The Singaporean claim: invented in the **1960s** by the "Four Heavenly Kings" chefs [S81]. Evidentiary state: the Malaysian account rests on contemporaries' anecdotes with **no interview with Loke (deceased), no documents, no artefacts**; Loke's own grandson (Anthony Loke, Malaysia's Transport Minister) said in 2024 that his grandfather brought the culture to Seremban but he could not say whether he was first in Malaysia [S81][S82]. Confidence: **disputed**; the Seremban claim is earlier and better-motivated but not proven. Both traditions descend from the older Guangdong/Jiangmen custom of eating raw fish on *renri* (人日), the seventh day of CNY.

**Relationships.** Mee sua ↔ yi mein: functional equivalents in different dialect traditions. Mee sua ↔ Foochow ang jiu chicken: the confinement/celebration dish. Lam mee ↔ mee sua: Nyonya vs Hokkien birthday-noodle. Yee sang: adjacent auspicious-food node.

## 20. Hakka Mee / Hakka Char Mee (客家麵)

**Names.** 客家麵 *Hakka mee*; in Ipoh and Seremban usage descended from **Tai Po mee** / **taipoo noodles** (大埔麵, from **Dabu county**, Guangdong) [S84]. Sarawak's descendant is **kolo mee** (乾撈麵, *kon lou mee*, "dry-tossed noodle"), also *mee kolok* [S83][S84].

**Etymology.** "Hakka noodle", after the dialect group. *Kolo* is a corruption of Cantonese/Hakka *kon lou* 乾撈, "dry tossed" [S83].

**Noodle type.** Thin, springy **wheat-and-egg noodle**, usually alkaline, blanched and tossed dry.

**Cultural origin.** **Hakka**, from **Dabu (Tai Po) county, Guangdong** [S84]. The peninsular expression is **Hakka mee**, concentrated in the Hakka tin towns — **Ipoh, Seremban, Ampang, Kuala Lumpur** [S84]. The Bornean expression is **kolo mee**, brought to **Kuching in the 1920s**, with **Kiew Shao Nyap**, a Hakka migrant from Guangdong, named as an early pioneer [S83][S84]. Sabah's **sang nyuk mee** *kon lau* (§2) is a third expression of the same dry-tossed logic. Confidence: **medium-high** — the Dabu ancestry is consistently reported; the Kiew Shao Nyap attribution is a single-lineage claim.

**Signature elements.** Noodles tossed in **lard and shallot oil** with light soy (peninsular Hakka mee often adds dark soy for colour; Sarawak kolo mee is characteristically **pale**, seasoned with lard, shallot oil, vinegar and white pepper, sometimes tinted red with *char siu* colouring); topped with **minced pork** braised with dried shiitake and sometimes dried shrimp; fried shallot or garlic; a few blanched greens; **soup on the side** — pork-bone broth with fishball or wonton. Pickled green chilli in soy is the standard condiment [S83][S84].

The **minced-pork topping over dry-tossed egg noodle with soup on the side** is the structural signature, and it is the same structure as **Soong Kee's Hakka beef noodle** in KL (§7c) [S29][S30] and as **dry bak chor mee** (§4) — three dishes, three dialect groups, one architecture, arrived at independently or through mutual borrowing. The graph should record this as a *shared-architecture* relation rather than descent.

**Penang relevance.** Hakka mee has a small Penang presence; the dominant dry-tossed egg-noodle dish in Penang is **wan tan mee** (§22c), the Cantonese equivalent, which occupies the same slot. Confidence: **medium-high**.

**Relationships.** Parent: Dabu/Tai Po mee. Siblings: **kolo mee** (Sarawak), **sang nyuk mee kon lau** (Sabah), Soong Kee-style dry Hakka beef noodle (KL). Architecture-siblings: dry bak chor mee (Teochew), wan tan mee (Cantonese).

## 21. Cantonese Wat Tan Hor / Moonlight Hor Fun

**Names.** 滑蛋河 *wat tan hor* (Cantonese), also 滑蛋河粉; Mandarin *huádàn hé* [S85][S86]. Also **char hor fun** (炒河粉) when the emphasis is on the fry. Malaysian menus: "wat tan hor", "wet hor fun", "Cantonese style hor fun". **Moonlight hor fun** is a distinct Singapore/Malaysian variant, 月光河 [S87].

**Etymology.** 滑 *wat* = "silky, slippery, smooth"; 蛋 *tan* = "egg"; 河 *hor* = *hor fun*. "Silky-egg hor fun" [S86]. **Moonlight**: named for the **raw egg yolk** set on top of the hot noodles, resembling a moon, stirred through by the diner [S87].

**Noodle type.** Flat **rice hor fun** (§12). The same gravy is routinely poured over **yee mee** (§11) — *wat tan yee mee* — or over crisp-fried egg noodle (*sang mee*), and Malaysian stalls offer the choice.

**Cultural origin.** **Cantonese**, Guangdong; the technique of finishing a stir-fry with a starch-thickened stock into which beaten egg is streamed is core Cantonese wok practice [S85][S86]. No named inventor and no useful origin legend, which is itself notable — wat tan hor is a *technique* dish, not a *story* dish. Confidence: **high** on Cantonese origin; the dish has no documented invention event.

**Signature elements.** The noodles are first fried hard and dry over high heat to build **wok hei** and char the edges — essential, and what separates a good wat tan hor from a bad one; then plated and covered with stock thickened with cornflour, into which beaten egg is drizzled in ribbons at the last second. Additions: pork or chicken slices, prawns, squid, fishcake, choy sum or kai lan, liver at some stalls. Condiments: **pickled green chilli** in light soy, and white pepper. Confidence: **high**.

**Regional variation.** Ipoh and the Klang Valley are the strongholds. Penang has it in Cantonese-run *tai chow* (大炒) stalls rather than as a signature hawker dish. **Moonlight hor fun** is the raw-yolk-on-top version, popularised in Singapore, best modelled as a **presentation variant** rather than a distinct dish [S87].

**Relationships.** Parent: Cantonese wok technique + Shahe fen. Siblings: *wat tan yee mee*, *sang mee* (crisp-noodle version), **Ipoh hor fun** (§12, same noodle, soup instead of fry). Variant: moonlight hor fun. Cousin: Indonesian *i fu mie* (yi mein under thick gravy) [S47].

## 22. Penang noodle dishes missing from the checklist

The following belong on any complete Penang noodle map and are not covered elsewhere in this dossier or in dossiers 01–03.

### 22a. Mee Sotong (Penang-only mamak fried noodle)
**Names.** Malay *mee sotong*, "squid noodle". A Tamil-Muslim (mamak) dish.
**Noodle type.** Yellow alkaline wheat mee.
**Origin.** A **Penang-specific variant of mee goreng mamak**, and by most accounts not found elsewhere in Malaysia in this form. The reference stall is **Hameed "Pata" Special Mee Sotong** at the Kota Selera food court, **Padang Kota Lama** (the Esplanade), by Fort Cornwallis. The family's account: Hameed's father began selling **mee rebus and mee goreng as early as 1942**; the mee sotong specialisation dates to **1978**; the stall is now run by the second generation, Shahul Hameed Syed Mohamed [S98][S99]. Confidence: **medium** (family-sourced, consistent across food media, four decades of continuous trade at one site).
**Signature elements.** Yellow noodles fried in a dark, sweet-spicy **squid sauce** built from a chilli-and-tomato base with the squid's own liquor; boiled potato, bean sprouts, egg; served with lime and cucumber; the squid is sliced thin and cooked into the sauce rather than added on top [S98][S99].
**Why it matters for the graph.** It is a **Penang mamak invention** occupying the same conceptual slot as Chinese char koay teow — a dark, sweet-savoury, wok-fried seafood noodle — arrived at through an entirely Indian-Muslim route. It is the clean counter-example to any model that treats Penang's fried-noodle culture as Chinese-only.

### 22b. Mee Udang
**Names.** Malay *mee udang*, "prawn noodle". Not to be confused with Chinese **Hokkien mee / hae mee**, also translatable as prawn noodle [S88].
**Noodle type.** Yellow mee, or bee hoon, or both; ordered *rebus* (soupy) or *goreng* (fried) [S88].
**Origin.** **Malay**, coastal Kedah/Penang. Timothy Tye's Penang Travel Tips describes it as "primarily a Malay dish similar to the Jawa Mee sold by Chinese hawkers", i.e. structurally related to **mee jawa / mee rebus** — a **tomato-and-chilli gravy** noodle — but built around whole large prawns [S88]. Confidence: **medium-high** on the Malay attribution and the mee jawa relationship; **low** on any origin date.
**The two Penang clusters:**
- **Teluk Kumbar**, on the island's southwest coast, is the historic centre; "Teluk Kumbar Mee Udang" has become a *style name* used by stalls all over the island, in the way "Penang Road famous" is used for cendol [S88].
- **Sungai Dua, Seberang Perai** (the mainland, on the road toward Kuala Muda and the Kedah border) is the second cluster, with roadside stalls such as **Aur Gading** and Ida serving very large prawns from the Muda estuary [S89]. Note the geography: Sungai Dua sits near the **Muda River**, the Penang–Kedah boundary and a productive prawn fishery, which is the obvious functional explanation for the cluster [S90].
**Signature elements.** Whole unpeeled prawns (size is the selling point); **tomato-based gravy** with chilli paste, shallot, garlic, tamarind; boiled egg; sliced green chilli; sometimes squid; a wedge of lime; bean sprouts [S88].
**Confidence on Sungai Dua origin claims: low.** No source located documents when or by whom mee udang was established at Sungai Dua. Food media treat it as an established roadside-food destination without history. **Flag as a research gap** — exactly the kind of recent, undocumented Malay-hawker culinary geography that Chinese hawker dishes get written up for and Malay ones do not.
**Relationships.** Siblings: **mee jawa / mee rebus** (Javanese-derived tomato-gravy noodle, dossier 02); **mee bandung Muar** (same family). Namesake-collision: Penang Hokkien mee / hae mee.

### 22c. Wan Tan Mee (雲吞麵)
**Names.** 雲吞麵 *wan tan mee* (Cantonese *wan tan min*), Mandarin *húntún miàn*; Malaysian spelling wantan mee / wan tan mee.
**Noodle type.** Thin round egg noodle — **youmian / mee kia** (§5, §15).
**Origin.** **Cantonese**, Guangzhou. The wonton-and-noodle pairing developed in 19th-century Guangzhou out of the much older *húntún* dumpling-soup tradition; carried to Malaya by Cantonese migrants [S96][S97]. Confidence: **high** for the general lineage; the "Tang dynasty" framing in food media refers to *húntún* the dumpling, not to the noodle dish.
**Penang variation.** The Malaysian dry style — noodles tossed in **dark soy, oyster sauce and pork lard**, topped with **char siu** and wontons, soup with more wontons and choy sum on the side — is standard [S96][S97]. Penang's local marker is a **chilli-sauce-forward** dressing at some stalls (the "red" wan tan mee), and pickled green chilli. Confidence: **medium** — regional wan tan mee differences are described impressionistically in Malaysian food media and are not well characterised.
**Relationships.** Architecture-siblings: Hakka mee (§20), kolo mee, dry bak chor mee (§4). Noodle-identity: mee kia = youmian.

### 22d. Koay Teow Th'ng — see §6.
Listed here for completeness: it is Penang's most-eaten Teochew noodle and is routinely omitted from "Penang food" lists in favour of char koay teow and asam laksa.

### 22e. Loh Bak, tau kwa and the side-dish layer
Not noodles, but structurally important: Penang **loh bak** (五香, five-spice pork rolls) stalls and **koay teow th'ng** / **koay chiap** stalls share the same *lou* braise and frequently the same customer visit. Any Penang noodle graph should carry an *eaten-with* edge type; loh bak, fried tofu, century egg and pickled vegetables sit on it.

### 22f. Deliberately excluded
Pasembur and rojak are salads, not noodles, though rojak shares **hae ko** with Penang chee cheong fun (§9) and can be linked by *shared-condiment*. Kuih are out of scope.

## Cross-cutting findings

1. **The naming layer is the most information-dense part of the system.** Three unrelated dishes called "Hokkien mee"; "mee pok" meaning the *wide* noodle; "you mee" with four referents; "koay chiap" versus "koay teow"; "chee cheong fun" containing no intestine; "Singapore noodles" from Hong Kong. A Penang culture graph that models only dishes and ingredients will lose most of what is interesting. It needs **name nodes distinct from dish nodes**, plus edge types for *same-name-different-dish*, *confused-with*, and *false-geographic-attribution*.

2. **Dialect group is a better predictor than nationality.** Teochew hawkers made clear broths and braised duck in Penang, Bangkok and Singapore alike; Hakka hawkers made dry-tossed minced-pork noodles in Ipoh, Kuching and Kota Kinabalu alike; Hokkien hawkers made starchy braised and prawn-stock noodles everywhere. The variation *within* a dialect tradition across three countries is often smaller than the variation *between* two dialect traditions in the same street.

3. **The substrate ingredient is the naturalisation marker.** Anchovy stock in pan mee; sayur manis in Sabah; hae ko in Penang chee cheong fun; tomato and lime in mamak bihun; dried flounder (*ti poh*) across northern Malaysia; sambal belacan served with everything. These are the points at which a Chinese dish becomes a Malaysian one, and they are more reliable evidence of local adaptation than any origin story.

4. **Evidence quality is systematically worse for Malay and mamak dishes than for Chinese ones.** Every Chinese dish here has at least a named founder story; mee udang at Sungai Dua has none, despite being a substantial commercial cluster. This is a bias in the *sources*, not in the dishes, and a graph built naively on available sources will inherit it.

5. **Claims to treat as unevidenced.** Ipoh limestone water → noodle texture. Boat noodles in 12th-century Ayutthaya. Boat-noodle broth colour as blood-concealment. Lam mee as a Cantonese mispronunciation of loh mee. Evaporated milk in fish soup as a Hainanese-British innovation. Chilli pan mee as a "traditional Hakka dish". Yi mein as the ancestor of instant noodles. Hainanese beef noodles as an import from Hainan.
